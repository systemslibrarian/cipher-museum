#### Elizebeth Smith Friedman

1892 – 1980

Elizebeth Smith worked at Riverbank Laboratories and married William Friedman. She later led U.S. Coast Guard cryptanalytic work against smugglers and wartime spy communications. Her achievements included training analysts and supplying expert evidence in prosecutions. The wartime work was a **team effort**, not a single-handed dismantling of every South American spy network.

##### Why This Person Matters

Friedman worked within government institutions, including the Coast Guard and Treasury. Secrecy and uneven attribution obscured parts of her contribution, but it was documented before the 2017 biography. For example, the NSA recognized her in its Cryptologic Hall of Honor and named a building for both Friedmans in 2002. See the [NSA biography](<https://www.nsa.gov/Press-Room/Digital-Media-Center/Biographies/Biography-View-Page/Article/1623028/elizebeth-s-friedman/>).

Historical Context

Arthur Scherbius patented the Enigma machine in 1918 as a commercial product. The German military adopted it in 1926 and progressively enhanced it throughout WWII. At its peak, the three-rotor Enigma I had 158,962,555,217,826,360,000 combinations of rotor order, starting positions, and ten plugboard pairs with ring settings fixed — and the naval M4 more still.

**The Polish breakthrough.** The story properly begins not at Bletchley but in Warsaw. In 1932 the young Polish mathematician **Marian Rejewski**, working for the Biuro Szyfrów with colleagues **Jerzy Różycki** and **Henryk Zygalski**, reconstructed the wiring of the German Army Enigma's rotors using intercepted traffic and compromised documents obtained through French intelligence. Rejewski did this by formulating message-key permutations as group-theoretic equations and solving them. By 1938 the Poles were reading roughly three-quarters of intercepted German Enigma traffic, using their own electromechanical machines (the *bomba kryptologiczna*, a predecessor using a different attack) and Zygalski's perforated sheets.

**The handover.** When Germany added two new rotors and changed procedures in late 1938, the Polish bureau's lead shrank. On **25 July 1939**, five weeks before the German invasion, Rejewski and his colleagues met British and French cryptanalysts in a forest near Pyry, south of Warsaw, and presented them with everything: the rotor wirings, the bomby, the Zygalski sheets, working Enigma replicas. The handover supplied crucial knowledge and equipment, building on earlier work in several countries.

**Hut 6 and Hut 8.** At Bletchley Park, **Hut 6** (under Gordon Welchman, then Stuart Milner-Barry) attacked German Army and Luftwaffe Enigma; **Hut 8** (under Alan Turing, then Hugh Alexander) attacked Naval Enigma — a far harder problem due to rigorous procedures and, from February 1942, the four-rotor M4 used by the U-boat fleet. Naval decrypts helped convoy routing and anti-submarine operations, alongside other intelligence and operational measures.

**Turing's bombe and Welchman's diagonal board.** Turing's bombe mechanised crib-based attacks: given a guessed plaintext fragment, it tested rotor settings electrically at the rate of thousands per minute, exploiting the loops in the implied permutation graph. Welchman’s diagonal board strengthened the constraint checking by exploiting reciprocal plugboard connections. Over 200 bombes were built, operated by Wrens working in shifts day and night.

**Strategic impact.** Sir Harry Hinsley, the official historian of British intelligence in WWII, estimated that ULTRA — the codename covering decrypts from Enigma and other systems — shortened the war in Europe by *at least* two years and "possibly four." This is a historical estimate rather than a measurable counterfactual. The value of particular decrypts varied by campaign and period.

*Sources: Hinsley, “British Intelligence in the Second World War,” HMSO 1979–1990; Kozaczuk, “Enigma,” 1984; Welchman, “The Hut Six Story,” 1982; Hodges, “Alan Turing: The Enigma,” 1983.*

How It Works

```text
Three-rotor Enigma (basic model):

1. Plugboard: swap 10 pairs of letters
   (150 trillion possible settings)

2. Three rotors: the rightmost advances
   before each letter; notches govern the others
   (17,576 rotor position combinations)

3. Reflector: sends signal back through
   rotors in reverse
   (this is Enigma's fatal flaw: a letter
   can never encrypt to itself)

4. Rotor selection: 3 from 5 rotors
   (60 rotor order combinations)

Total: ~158 quintillion settings
But: same letter never encrypts to itself → cribs work
```

How It Was Broken

Crib-Based Cryptanalysis (Turing's Bombe)

Complexity: Computationally intensive · Required dedicated machines

Enigma's fatal flaw: a letter can never encrypt to itself (the reflector guarantees this). If you know (or guess) a plaintext word — called a crib — you can eliminate all configurations where any crib letter would encrypt to itself. Weather vocabulary could supply useful guessed plaintext, though message formats varied. A correct crib supplies constraints for key search, while an incorrect guess can fail to produce a valid candidate. The Bombe machine tested the remaining configurations electromechanically at thousands per minute.

Operator Errors

Human factors

Predictable plaintext, repeated settings, captured material, and operating procedures gave attackers useful information. Machine structure mattered too: the reflector’s no-self-encipherment property helped reject impossible cribs. It is misleading to attribute the breaks exclusively to human error rather than mathematics.

##### 1 · Self-encryption rejection

Enigma's reflector means no letter can ever encrypt to itself. This fact lets you reject impossible candidate alignments of a crib without testing rotor settings: if any letter of the crib lines up with the same letter in the ciphertext, that placement is impossible.

The Erskine Messages — All Three Now Broken

Three naval M4 signals intercepted by HMS *Hurricane* in the North Atlantic in 1942 went unread at the time. Ralph Erskine reprinted them in a 1995 letter to *Cryptologia* as an open challenge, and for a decade they were the best-known still-unsolved Enigma traffic.

In January 2006 Stefan Krah launched the **M4 Message Breaking Project**, a distributed-computing effort combining brute-force rotor search with hill-climbing on the plugboard. The first signal fell on **20 February 2006** — Kptlt. Hartwig Looks of *U-264*, forced under by depth charges. The second fell on **7 March 2006** — a routine sighting-and-weather report signed Schroeder, matching the war diary of *U-623*.

The third resisted for another seven years. It was broken in **January 2013** by Dan Girard, with Michael Hörenberg, and proved to be a fuel-and-provisions report from Kptlt. Hermann Rasch of *U-106*. **None of the three remains unsolved.**

These later solutions combined historical knowledge, cryptanalytic search and substantial computation. They do not establish that every surviving Enigma message is solvable under the same conditions.

*Sources: Stefan Krah, [M4 Message Breaking Project ↗](<https://www.bytereef.org/m4_project.html>); Michael Hörenberg, [Breaking German Navy Ciphers ↗](<https://enigma.hoerenberg.com/index.php?cat=M4+Project+2006>); John Leyden, “Codebreakers unravel second Enigma intercept,” *The Register*, 15 March 2006; Ralph Erskine, letter to *Cryptologia*, 1995.*

#### Why This Matters

Fialka M-125 entered service in 1956 and was used by the Soviet Union and allied countries. Variants had different keyboards and country-specific rotor wiring. It was one of several communications-security systems, not the device through which every Warsaw Pact signal passed.

The machine changed several features familiar from Enigma, including the wheel count, stepping, and input substitution. Improvements to particular weaknesses do not prove that a design has no others.

This exhibit establishes no public cryptanalytic break of operational traffic. That historical record does not establish security today; this obsolete system is presented for historical study.

How It Works

Adjacent rotors move in opposite directions. Mechanical pin patterns control irregular stepping; not every wheel necessarily advances on each keypress. The punched card supplies a substitution rather than setting the rotor order, ring positions, or stepping pins. See the [Crypto Museum’s documented machines and technical reference](<https://www.cryptomuseum.com/crypto/fialka/>).

Three further design choices matter:

- **Self-encipherment is possible.** Fialka’s electrical arrangement permits a letter to encipher to itself, unlike military Enigma.

- **Punched-card substitution.** A daily card replaces the plugboard’s role; the other machine settings remain separate.

- **Thirty-contact alphabet.** The later M-125-3M supports mixed letters, numbers, and punctuation through operating modes; 30 contacts do not individually represent all those symbols at once.

#### Why This Matters

Félix Delastelle developed Bifid and Trifid fractionation and the Four-square digram substitution. The Four-square is his digraphic cousin to Playfair: encrypt letters two at a time, but use two keyed alphabets instead of one. It avoids Playfair's "same row / same column / same letter" edge cases and the needed insertion of X between doubled letters.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-scroll"></use></svg>
```

Historical Context

Published in his 1902 book Traité Élémentaire de Cryptographie, the Four-square joined the Bifid and Trifid in Delastelle's portfolio of methods built on the Polybius square. The modern procedure is documented by cipher organisations and teaching implementations; this exhibit does not establish a complete military-use history.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-gear"></use></svg>
```

How It Works

Arrange four 5×5 squares (J merged into I) in a 2×2 grid. The top-left and bottom-right are plain alphabets; the top-right and bottom-left are keyed.

```text
┌─────────────┬─────────────┐
│  PLAIN A→Z  │  KEY 1      │
├─────────────┼─────────────┤
│  KEY 2      │  PLAIN A→Z  │
└─────────────┴─────────────┘
```

To encrypt a digram: locate the first letter in the top-left and the second in the bottom-right. Read the cipher digram from the top-right (row of letter 1, column of letter 2) and the bottom-left (row of letter 2, column of letter 1).

ℹ️

**About this demo:** To guarantee that Decrypt exactly restores your original message, this demo reversibly escapes the letters X, Q, and Z (as ZA, ZB, ZC) before enciphering, so padding and filler X can never be confused with a real letter. Messages containing X, Q, or Z therefore produce slightly different ciphertext than a strictly classical four-square cipher would. Odd-length messages are padded with a trailing X.

How It Was Broken

Digram Frequency Analysis

Complexity: Moderate

A fixed four-square key substitutes digrams. Non-overlapping plaintext-pair counts are therefore relabelled, while individual letter counts need not become uniform. Language scores can guide key searches, but required text length depends on the message and attack.

Simulated Annealing

Complexity: Moderate

Hill climbing and simulated annealing test candidate square pairs using language scores. They can become trapped in poor candidates; runtime and success depend on the search budget, text length and scoring model. This page establishes no universal seconds-long recovery guarantee.

#### Why This Matters

Fractionated Morse changes the units being substituted: each ciphertext letter represents a triple drawn from dots, dashes and separators. Boundaries need not coincide with plaintext letters, but Morse and language structure still constrain the result.

- Convert plaintext to Morse code (dots, dashes, letter separators)

#### The GCHQ Trio — Ellis, Cocks, and Williamson

1970 – 1974

James Ellis’s classified report on the possibility of non-secret encryption is dated **January 1970**. Clifford Cocks proposed an RSA-related construction in 1973, and Malcolm Williamson developed finite-field key agreement in 1974. Their work remained classified while public academic research independently developed public-key cryptography. Public disclosure of the GCHQ work came in 1997.

##### Why This Person Matters

The episode distinguishes private discovery from publication and public adoption. Public-key methods became central to internet authentication and key establishment, but a mathematical proposal is not the same as a deployed, authenticated, securely encoded protocol. Counterfactual claims that earlier publication would have advanced the entire field by an exact number of years cannot be established.

#### Why This Matters

While Lorenz SZ40/42 (“Tunny”) carried Hitler’s strategic traffic, the Siemens & Halske T52 — codenamed “Sturgeon” at Bletchley Park — carried Luftwaffe and Wehrmacht command traffic over leased Swedish telephone lines from 1940. Swedish access to those circuits supplied material for cryptanalysis.

In 1940 Beurling worked with the Swedish Defence Staff’s cryptographic service, the predecessor of FRA as an independent agency. His approximately two-week breakthrough, assisted by Hans Rudberg and Bertil Nyman, was followed by continuing key-recovery and decryption work. The [FRA account](<https://fra.se/omfra/frashistoria/gskrivarenocharnebeurling.4.55af049f184e92956c42a59.html>) distinguishes these stages.

How It Works

The T52 family used teleprinter ciphers; later T52d/e models added irregular stepping. Plaintext is encoded in 5-bit Baudot characters and combined with a keystream generated by ten wheels with pairwise-coprime lengths (47, 53, 59, 61, 64, 65, 67, 69, 71, 73). The following two-bank description belongs to this teaching model, not a wiring specification for every T52 variant:

- **Five wheels generate an additive keystream** that shifts each character.

- **Five wheels select, per character, one of several keyed substitution permutations** applied after the additive shift.

In the original T52 the substitution is a bit-permutation on the 5-bit Baudot code. The educational demo above operates on the 26-letter alphabet using mod-26 addition and six keyed permutations of A–Z, which preserves the architectural lesson — additive keystream plus per-character permutation, ten coprime-length wheels — while keeping the round-trip clean for arbitrary text.

How It Was Broken

Beurling’s reconstruction (1940)

Initial reconstruction in about two weeks; assistants and later key-recovery work also mattered

FRA publishes Carl-Gösta Borelius’s later reconstruction of how Beurling may have proceeded. Borelius describes messages sent with matching settings, frequent letter-shift and space characters, and regularities preserved when bit addition and permutation act on parallel messages. He explicitly presents a hypothesis, not Beurling’s own step-by-step account. See FRA, [“Hur gjorde Arne Beurling för att forcera G-skrivaren?”](<https://www.fra.se/omfra/frashistoria/forceringenavgskrivaren.4.55af049f184e92956c42acb.html>). Letter shift and space were useful statistical clues within this reconstruction; additive masking still had to be accounted for.

App, the decryption apparatus

Purpose: process traffic after suitable keys were recovered

Beurling and engineer Vigo Lindstein developed decryption equipment known as “appar.” It helped process messages once suitable keys had been recovered; it should not be described as automatically finding every daily setting. This exhibit does not establish that all resulting intelligence was shared with the Allies.

Historical Context

Antoine Rossignol became a prominent French royal cryptologist in the seventeenth century. Kahn places the celebrated Réalmont decipherment in 1628, not 1626. Antoine and, later, his son Bonaventure developed and operated royal nomenclators; a single invention date should not be assigned to all of these changing keys.

The Rossignols' code book contained roughly **587 numbers**, each standing for a syllable, a common word, a single letter, or — critically — nothing at all. Some numbers were pure decoys (nulls); Kahn describes one sign that erased the preceding sign. Bonaventure’s son Antoine-Bonaventure also entered the family’s cryptanalytic profession. By 1891, historians studying Catinat’s campaigns had ciphered dispatches they could not read and asked Bazeries for help.

In the 1890s Étienne Bazeries reconstructed a Rossignol-era nomenclator by testing letter and syllable readings against repeated groups and the correspondence’s context. Kahn describes homophones, nulls and an instruction to erase the preceding sign. A deciphered 1691 dispatch led Bazeries to identify General Bulonde with the Man in the Iron Mask, but that historical inference remains disputed.

How It Was Broken

Frequency Analysis on Syllables

Complexity: Hard · Took Bazeries ~3 years

Bazeries tested syllable interpretations, including groups for *les ennemis*, and used partial readings to extend the reconstruction. Different groups could represent the same syllable or ending; some entries represented individual letters or whole words. A single frequency table was therefore insufficient.

The "Delete" Trap

Complexity: Special codebook instructions

Kahn reports a sign that erased the preceding sign. Such instructions complicate a solver’s model, but the source does not establish that one erasure code alone explains the long delay in decipherment. The browser demo uses its own explicitly defined erase-one-letter convention.

Modern Cryptanalytic Tools

Complexity: Depends on text, model and search budget

Computer search can test candidate codebooks with language scores, but success depends on the corpus, segmentation, nulls and special instructions. No timed recovery of the historical Rossignol codebook is demonstrated here; the small generated example does not establish a minutes-long attack on the original system.

The Man in the Iron Mask

A dispatch of 8 July 1691 concerned the imprisonment of Vivien Labbé, Seigneur du Bulonde, after his withdrawal from the siege of Cuneo. Two otherwise unattested groups appeared in the passage about his permitted movements at Pignerol. Bazeries interpreted them as referring to a mask. Bazeries believed this was the famous Iron Mask. The identification remains disputed, including the interpretation of the numbers supposedly denoting a mask. David Kahn revisited the evidence in *Cryptologia* 29(1), pp. 43–49 (2005), “The Man in the Iron Mask — Encore et Enfin, Cryptologically.” The letter is evidence of the cipher’s decipherment, not a confirmed solution to the prisoner’s identity.

Bazeries reconstructed 587 equivalents; this widget does not reproduce that historical key. This demo uses a teaching-scale codebook — 50 common digrams + 26 letters + 6 nulls + 1 "delete previous" trap (83 codewords) — deterministically generated from the Codebook Seed.

The cipher traditionally attributed to Count Gronsfeld adapts the Vigenère cipher to use numeric keys instead of letter keys. Historical Context

Boris Hagelin developed the C-series at AB Cryptoteknik in Sweden during the 1930s. The C-36 belongs to the early pin-and-lug family; the later C-38 became the US Army’s [M-209](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/m209.html>). How It Works

Each wheel carries active and inactive pins. Their positions determine which guide arms engage the lugs attached to cage bars. As the operator turns the handle, displaced bars contribute to a numerical key value. A changing shift combines that value with the input letter.

For a simplified reciprocal letter-subtractor convention, absorb any alphabet offset into K, number A–Z as 0–25 and compute `C = (K − P) mod 26`. Applying the same K sequence to C recovers P. The physical alphabet arrangement and offset must also be specified for a particular machine. The difficult part is generating K, not the subtraction. A long wheel cycle alone does not guarantee that the resulting shifts lack exploitable structure.

[Try the M-209 encrypt/decrypt demo](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/m209.html>) for the museum’s pin-and-lug interactive. It illustrates a related machine, rather than pretending to reproduce every C-36 or C-52 configuration.

How It Was Broken

H. Paul Greenough published a **known-plaintext attack in 1999** against the C-52 and similar machines with irregular pinwheel stepping. The paper’s abstract describes recovering the subset of wheels, their order, and active pin positions. This exhibit summarizes that stated scope; it does not reproduce the full attack or establish a universal data requirement. This is a public cryptanalytic result, distinct from a claim that every machine sold under the family name shared one weakness.

The [M-209 exhibit](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/m209.html>) covers wartime known-plaintext and statistical attacks on that earlier branch. Public attacks exist against members of the cipher family; those results are not a blanket statement about identical internals or attacks across all variants.

Crypto AG and the Supply Chain

William Friedman’s declassified reports document US intelligence contact with Hagelin, discussions of C-52 variants, and attention to which customers received which machines. Later, Crypto AG was secretly owned by the CIA and West German BND from 1970 as part of the operation known as Rubicon. Selected export designs and procedures were influenced to make foreign traffic readable.

The company history must not be projected backwards onto every C-36. A wartime C-36, a publicly attacked C-52 configuration, and a deliberately weakened later export product are different claims. The lesson is that secret keys cannot repair a supplier’s deliberately constrained design or operating procedure.

Pin-and-lug machines turn a physical key into a stream of shifts, making key generation, key reuse, and biased output visible. Crypto AG adds the need for independent design review. A trusted brand, an impressive mechanical period, and a confidential sales process are not substitutes for public cryptanalysis.

#### Why This Matters

Lester Hill’s cipher illustrates block encryption through matrix multiplication. Its linear structure also gives a particularly clear known-plaintext attack. AES uses linear algebra in MixColumns, but the complete AES cipher includes nonlinear operations and is not simply a larger Hill cipher.

Historical Context

Lester S. Hill published his matrix-based system in the *American Mathematical Monthly* in 1929. It maps groups of letters through an invertible matrix modulo the alphabet size. The system provides a useful introduction to linear algebra in cryptography without establishing a direct historical influence on every later matrix-based design.

Hill also explored mechanical implementation. The cryptanalytic limitation illustrated here follows from the linear equations themselves; no assertion about widespread deployment is needed to demonstrate it.

How It Works

Convert letters to numbers (A=0, B=1…Z=25). Arrange plaintext in column vectors of length n. Multiply by an invertible n×n key matrix modulo 26.

```text
Key matrix K (2×2):     Plaintext: HI = [7, 8]
| 3  3 |
| 2  5 |    C = K × P mod 26
                        = [3×7+3×8, 2×7+5×8] mod 26
                        = [45, 54] mod 26
                        = [19, 2]
                        = TC
```

Hill cipher: plaintext vector multiplied by key matrix mod 26 — HI becomes TC (this is the demo's default key)

Decryption uses the matrix inverse: P = K⁻¹ × C mod 26. Not all matrices have inverses mod 26 — the key matrix must be chosen carefully.

ℹ️

**About this demo:** To guarantee that Decrypt exactly restores your original message, this demo reversibly escapes the letters X, Q, and Z (as ZA, ZB, ZC) before enciphering, so padding and filler X can never be confused with a real letter. Messages containing X, Q, or Z therefore produce slightly different ciphertext than a strictly classical Hill cipher would. Odd-length messages are padded with a trailing X, and the key matrix must be invertible mod 26 — the demo rejects singular matrices.

How It Was Broken

Known Plaintext Attack

Complexity: Easy with suitable known blocks

For an n×n Hill key, collect n matched plaintext/ciphertext *blocks* as the columns of matrices P and C. When P is invertible modulo 26 — that is, gcd(det(P), 26) = 1 — the key follows directly: K = C × P⁻¹ mod 26. Merely knowing n blocks does not guarantee this condition. If it fails, more observations or a more general solution of the linear congruences may be needed. The cipher remains vulnerable because the relation is linear.

**Enough information:** the two blocks HI and LL give P = [[7, 11], [8, 11]], whose determinant is −11, coprime to 26. Matched with ciphertext TC and OZ, they recover K = [[3, 3], [2, 5]], the demo’s default key.

**Not enough information:** AA and AB both map to AA and DF under either K = [[3, 3], [2, 5]] or K = [[5, 3], [2, 5]]. Both keys are valid, but these two known blocks do not distinguish them. These examples use classical A = 0 blocks; remember the demo’s X/Q/Z escaping when analyzing other inputs.

Reading: Joshua Holden, [*The Mathematics of Secrets: Cryptography from Caesar Ciphers to Digital Encryption*](<https://ciphermuseum.com/further-reading.html#holden>) (2017), §§1.6–1.7. The invertibility check above specifies when the direct matrix-inverse recovery works.

Cryptography vs Steganography

Encryption aims to hide a message’s meaning from someone who sees its ciphertext; steganography aims to hide the presence of a message. Herodotus describes concealment without specifying an encryption algorithm. Finding the concealed marks and interpreting them are separate tasks. Modern systems can encrypt a payload before embedding it in cover material.

The Modern Heir: Steganography Today

Digital steganography can embed information in images or audio, while covert channels can exploit other observable behaviour. Security depends on what an observer can detect, inspect and modify. An overlooked channel is not automatically secure, and finding a hidden payload need not reveal its meaning if it is separately encrypted.

#### Why This Matters

Homophonic substitution was the Renaissance cryptographers’ answer to frequency analysis: giving common letters multiple cipher symbols can flatten individual-symbol frequencies, although language patterns can still support cryptanalysis.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-scroll"></use></svg>
```

Historical Context

European diplomatic ciphers used multiple substitutes for common letters; David Kahn documents a Mantuan example from 1401. This does not establish a direct transmission of al-Kindi’s methods to Renaissance cryptographers. The idea: if common letters betray themselves by appearing too often, give them multiple disguises. The letter E might encrypt as 12, 37, or 44 — chosen at random each time. With a suitable allocation and random selection, the frequency of each common letter is spread across its substitutes.

This system was widely used in European diplomatic correspondence from the 15th through 17th centuries. The Vatican, Italian city-states, and royal courts all employed variations. Some systems used over 100 symbols.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-gear"></use></svg>
```

How It Works

Assign multiple ciphertext symbols to each plaintext letter, weighted by letter frequency. Common letters get more symbols; rare letters get fewer.

```text
E → 12, 37, 44, 71, 83  (5 symbols, ~12.7% freq)
T → 21, 90             (2 symbols, ~9.1% freq)
A → 05, 63             (2 symbols, ~8.2% freq)
Z → 99                 (1 symbol,  ~0.07% freq)
```

Homophonic substitution: E gets 5 symbols, Z gets 1 — more symbols for common letters masks frequency patterns

To encrypt: for each plaintext letter, randomly pick one of its assigned symbols. This illustrative allocation reduces some peaks but does not make frequencies uniform: with equal choice among alternatives, each E symbol has an expected frequency of 12.7% / 5 = 2.54%, each T symbol 9.1% / 2 = 4.55%, and the Z symbol 0.07%. Approximate flattening requires the number of substitutes to track the plaintext distribution; a short message can still vary substantially.

Structural Pattern Analysis

Complexity: Moderate

Even with flattened frequencies, the cipher retains structural patterns. Digraph frequencies (two-symbol sequences) still reflect English digraph statistics — TH, HE, IN, ER appear more often than XX, QQ, ZZ. With suitable ciphertext, statistical scoring and search can recover mappings; success depends on the language, message length, symbol allocation, and search budget.

Poor Symbol Distribution

Complexity: Easy if symbols not perfectly weighted

An uneven allocation or biased choice of substitutes can leave strong frequency peaks. Such a peak suggests hypotheses; it does not by itself identify the underlying plaintext letter.

#### How Many Codes Is the Reader Facing?

A homophonic key is usually described by how many code symbols it holds, and that number does the work. With 26 plaintext letters and at most five homophones each, one fixed key has **at most 130 code types**, so each code symbol carries at most **log<sub>2</sub> 130 ≈ 7.022 bits** — and only if all 130 were equally likely, which proportional allocation does not achieve.

Research collections are built by encrypting many texts, each under its own independently generated key. Pool all that ciphertext and the measured alphabet becomes much larger: eleven thousand codes gives log<sub>2</sub> 11000 ≈ 13.425 bits. **That is a statistic about the collection, not about the letter in front of you.** An attacker who holds one message still faces one key with its own small set of codes. Growing the pool cannot make a single message harder to read, and a cryptanalytic result that improves as the pool grows is measuring the pool.

The worked, fully enumerable version of this — and why a unicity estimate neither certifies a proposed reading nor promises an efficient solver — is in [the entropy exhibit](<https://ciphermuseum.com/hall-of-foundations/entropy.html#pooled-entropy>).

#### Why This Matters

This exhibit is about a body of real ciphertext and the man who read it. In 2001 the historian Tom Mahon found roughly three hundred documents containing encrypted messages in a Dublin archive — the papers of Moss Twomey, IRA chief of staff from 1926 to 1936. Between them they held about 1,300 individual cryptograms, mostly dispatches between headquarters and organisers in Britain and the United States, dating from the 1920s. Mahon brought in the American cryptanalyst Jim Gillogly, and together they published *Decoding the IRA* in 2008. Their book left one challenge unresolved; Richard Bean subsequently solved it in 2019.

Historical Context

The collection documents IRA correspondence during the 1920s. Dunin and Schmeh describe both columnar transpositions and a smaller set of Vigenère-encrypted passages embedded in otherwise readable letters, including an example dated 4 May 1923. The collection should not be described as using only one cipher.

For the Vigenère passages, Gillogly combined short samples to search for a reused key. The recovered keyword, GVZKLG, is TEAPOT transformed by a reversed alphabet. Ampersands represented Z in the ciphertext. This case shows why reused keys and operator conventions matter alongside the underlying cipher.

How the Cipher Worked

Most of the cryptograms used [columnar transposition](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/columnar.html>), the mechanism demonstrated here. The message is written into a grid of fixed line length; the columns are then read out in an order set by a keyword. No letter is ever replaced — every letter of the plaintext survives into the ciphertext, only its position changes. Single-letter frequencies cannot recover the column order, but they can help suggest a transposition and a likely language. Letter-group statistics help score candidate orders.

Operators also padded. In the message Gillogly works through as an example, the encipherer had inserted two columns of meaningless vowels to disguise the shape of the grid.

The demo above uses the museum's columnar engine. It is the right mechanism, but it is a clean-room model: it does not reproduce the padding conventions, the five-letter grouping, or the handwriting of the originals.

How It Was Broken

Hill climbing over column orders

Method: scored search over candidate column orders

Gillogly wrote hill-climbing software aimed at columnar transposition. In the worked example described by Dunin and Schmeh, he tried line lengths in the range of about eight to fifteen characters, ran the search around a hundred times from different random starting keys, and watched for the point at which recognisable English words began to fall out of a candidate solution. The approach is the same family this museum covers under [hill climbing](<https://ciphermuseum.com/cryptanalysis.html#hill-climbing>), but its success depends on message length, language scoring, transcription and the particular cipher conventions.

#### Why This Matters

Jefferson described a wheel cipher in the 1790s. Bazeries later developed a similar cylinder independently, and the American work of Parker Hitt and Joseph Mauborgne led to the M-94, adopted in the early 1920s. Reinvention demonstrates the appeal of the mechanism, not a proof of its security.

Historical Context

Jefferson’s manuscript describes the device but does not establish that he built or deployed it. Bazeries’s later cylinder and the Hitt–Mauborgne development should be identified separately rather than collapsing the whole M-94 invention into its adoption year. The [Signal Corps historical account](<https://www.nsa.gov/Portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/friedman-documents/publications/FOLDER_205/41762559080174.pdf>) discusses the independent designs.

Jefferson proposed a cylinder with 36 alphabet disks. The later M-94 used 25 disks and enciphered 25-letter blocks; longer messages needed multiple blocks. Its Army and Navy service histories should not be reduced to a claim that one replacement date applied to every U.S. military branch.

#### Why This Matters

JN-25 was not a simple substitution cipher. It combined a large operational codebook with a changing additive mask layered on top of each five-digit group. Breaking it required reconstructing both layers while traffic was still flowing. The resulting intelligence shaped convoy defense, carrier deployments, and the Midway ambush that shifted the Pacific war.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-scroll"></use></svg>
```

Historical Context

From 1939 onward, the Imperial Japanese Navy used JN-25 variants for fleet operations. Messages were first converted into five-digit code groups from a shared codebook. Operators then added a second five-digit number from additive tables, digit by digit modulo 10, to conceal the underlying code group frequency.

US Navy cryptanalysts at Station HYPO (Pearl Harbor), OP-20-G (Washington), and British and Australian partner sites worked these intercepts continuously. They rarely had complete coverage, but even partial recovery of repeated logistics terms and operation markers provided high-value strategic warning.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-gear"></use></svg>
```

How It Works

- Encode operational words and phrases into five-digit code groups from a codebook.

- Take a five-digit additive from a separate key list.

- Add each digit modulo 10 to create transmitted ciphertext groups.

- Receiver subtracts the same additive list to recover code groups and decode the message.

The demo on this page is a pedagogical miniature: letter-level code groups with additive superencipherment. It illustrates the two linked tasks of recovering additive information and reconstructing codebook meanings. Patterns across related ciphertexts can guide that work before a complete additive table is known.

How It Was Broken
