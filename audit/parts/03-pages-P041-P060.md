### Colossus

Electronic counting accelerated the statistical search behind the Tunny breakthrough.

Codebreaking machine; synthetic two-wheel setting experiment, not full decryption.

#### Why This Matters

Colossus helped attack Lorenz teleprinter traffic, known at Bletchley Park as Tunny. Tommy Flowers and his team built it at the Post Office Research Station. The first machine was working at Bletchley Park in February 1944.

#### How the Search Worked

Punched paper tape supplied ciphertext. Electronic logic evaluated combinations of tape data and generated wheel patterns, while counters accumulated statistical scores. This accelerated the search for Lorenz chi-wheel settings. Colossus supported a larger cryptanalytic process; it did not automatically turn arbitrary intercepted traffic into readable messages.

#### Tape Clock → Condition → Count

Colossus read intercepted ciphertext from a continuous paper-tape loop. Sprocket holes supplied the timing signals for counting and electronic wheel movement. The wheel patterns were generated electronically, replacing the second synchronized tape used by Heath Robinson. At about 5,000 tape characters per second, the reader supplied many observations for each statistical trial; that figure is not 5,000 keys or completed trials per second.

- **Clock:** a tape timing pulse presents the next character and coordinates the electronic pattern.

- **Condition:** configured logic combines selected tape and wheel signals.

- **Count:** a counter increments when that condition is satisfied.

- **Select:** at the end of the count, a configured “set total” can select scores for printing.

The original worked example below isolates one counter. Let A and B be two selected binary inputs to the logic, and count positions where `A XOR B = 0`. The six positions and their values are invented teaching data, not historical traffic or a full Colossus simulation.

A selected score is evidence to investigate, not a recovered plaintext.

The machine had five separate counters, allowing several conditions to be counted together subject to shared stepping and spanning controls. This example shows only one condition. Count dots (zeroes) in delta D1 XOR delta D2, equivalently delta Z1 XOR delta Z2 XOR delta chi1 XOR delta chi2. Impulses 1 and 2 are the two low bits in this five-bit representation. All 41 × 31 = 1,271 candidate settings are counted.

This is synthetic five-bit traffic with an explicitly planted residual statistic: the parity of D1 XOR D2 changes with probability 0.30 before added noise. The known wheel patterns are synthetic. #### Historical Context and Limits

Bill Tutte reconstructed the cipher’s logical structure, enabling later machine-assisted methods. Colossus was programmable through controls and connections, but was neither a stored-program computer nor a general-purpose machine. Its role concerned Lorenz, not Enigma. The National Museum of Computing houses a working reconstruction.

Limits: widths 2–12, at most 20,000 score evaluations, including adjacency preprocessing, phase-transition scoring and every restart. Unknown-width mode divides one total budget across widths 2–12; preprocessing may exhaust a width's allocation. Columnar transposition rearranges positions while preserving letter counts. It is a useful way to study the difference between a large number of possible keys and the statistical structure an attacker can exploit.

Historical Context

Columnar systems appear in historical military and recreational cryptography, sometimes combined with other operations. “Columnar transposition” describes a family of writing and reading conventions, not one securely dated nineteenth-century invention. Complete rectangles, incomplete final rows and padding rules produce different attack problems.

How It Works

Write the message in rows across a grid whose width is the keyword length. Number the columns by alphabetical order of the keyword letters. Read off columns in that numbered order. [Myszkowski transposition](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/myszkowski.html>) changes how repeated keyword letters are handled: tied columns are read together across each row.

Analysts try plausible widths and rearrange columns, looking for useful letter contacts and readable text. #### Why This Matters

Commercial codebooks replaced frequently used business expressions with compact codewords to reduce telegraph charges. Formats and billing rules changed over time. A phrase could become a single code group, but a public book did not thereby provide secrecy.

Commercial codes included ABC, Lieber’s and Bentley’s. Kahn describes the 1903 international rules permitting pronounceable artificial words up to ten letters, and the subsequent combination of two five-letter groups within that allowance. Bentley’s *Complete Phrase Code* followed in 1906. A universal five-letter CVCVC tariff rule is therefore misleading.

Private codebooks and additional encipherment could obscure the codeword meanings. Their protection depended on access to the book, traffic patterns and the added cipher, not simply on whether a printed volume was labelled private.

How It Works

A two-part codebook can provide two lookup directions; historical layouts varied:

- A **plain index** listing every English word or phrase the codebook covers, in alphabetical order, each beside its codeword.

- A **code index** listing every codeword in alphabetical order, each beside its plaintext.

The museum’s CVCVC pattern uses 20 consonants (excluding Y) and five vowels, allowing 20³ × 5² = 200,000 combinations. If all 21 consonants were allowed, the count would be 231,525. Neither count is the number of entries in a particular historical book. Error-detecting designs restrict the vocabulary so that likely transmission errors are less likely to produce another valid codeword.

The demo above operates on a small fixed wordlist using exactly the CVCVC encoding scheme. Words not in the wordlist fall through to a per-letter codebook so the round-trip stays clean for arbitrary input.

Where the Confidentiality Came From (and Where It Failed)

Public codebooks: no confidentiality at all

Complexity: Lookup

Anyone with a copy of ABC or Bentley’s could read public-codebook traffic. Their job was compression and error-checking, not secrecy.

Private codebooks: tractable for state actors

Complexity: Within reach of professional cryptanalytic bureaus

A fixed one-to-one phrase code preserves repeated groups and can expose relationships between messages. Known business context can help infer entries. The volume and type of traffic, use of alternatives and any super-encipherment affect how much can be recovered; no universal recovery time follows.

Codebook + super-encipherment

Security depends on the added cipher and key handling

An additional cipher can protect codegroups, but it must be analysed on its own terms. Additive reuse can expose relationships, as in some military code systems. That analogy does not show that every private commercial code used the same construction or had the same weakness.

Historical Context

A crucial discovery was that unaccented Roman letters represented **word spaces**, while other signs encoded the text. They were not arbitrary meaningless filler. Grouping symbols by their contexts, testing likely words, and examining German spelling helped the researchers work out the system.

How It Works

The manuscript uses homophones, word separators, and signs for some letter groups and concepts. Its roughly 90 cipher signs do not divide uniformly among 26 letters. Computational and Linguistic Analysis (2011)

#### Why This Matters

By 1778 George Washington’s army had been forced out of New York City and the British held the harbour, Manhattan, and most of Long Island. Washington needed reliable, sustained intelligence from inside that perimeter — ship arrivals, troop movements, the names of ranking British officers in the city. He turned to a 24-year-old Continental Army officer, Major Benjamin Tallmadge, who in late 1778 organised what is now called the Culper Spy Ring.

Tallmadge’s solution to the secrecy problem was a numerical codebook with roughly 760 numbered entries. Each entry was a person, place, military term, or common word. “Gen. Washington” became `711` and “New York” became `727`. Tallmadge, Washington, Woodhull and Townsend were among its users; this page does not establish an exhaustive distribution list. It was used continuously from 1779 until the end of the war — and no British break of the cipher itself is recorded. Surviving copies of Tallmadge’s codebook are in the Library of Congress.

How It Works

The codebook is a parallel-list nomenclator. The plaintext list runs alphabetically through the words and proper names of interest; each entry has a number from 1 to roughly 760. The cipher list runs in numeric order, each number against its plaintext, so the recipient can decode.

Operators wrote messages using ordinary English for low-value content and substituted code numbers for sensitive words — names of officers, ship counts, place names. Numbers were embedded in otherwise innocuous letters (“I saw 711 yesterday and he asked after the 178 in 727” — “I saw Washington yesterday and he asked after the enemy in New York”).

Because the word list is alphabetical, an entry's number tells you roughly where its word falls in the alphabet. Entries 1–710 are common words; 711–763 are the proper nouns. Read straight from the codebook:

| Code | Meaning |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 178 | enemy | sits in the alphabetical run 175 *embark* · 176 *employ* · 179 *example* |
| 355 | lady | the entry behind the "Agent 355" legend — a word, not a person |
| 596 | secret |  |
| 711 | Gen. Washington | first of the proper nouns |
| 727 | New York |  |

In the consulted edited transcription, 178 is *enemy*, not *spy*. The list contains many s-words after 606 *speaker*; 756 *Spain* belongs to the separate proper-name section. Transcription gaps and printing errors make the original manuscript important for claims about the complete vocabulary.

For deeper concealment, Culper messages were written in James Jay’s invisible ink (“sympathetic stain”) between the lines of cover letters. Invisible ink concealed the presence of writing, a different purpose from replacing words with numbers.

The demo above uses a stable 215-entry codebook indexed from 100. Words not in the codebook fall through to per-letter codes in the 800s, bracketed by sentinels 998 and 999 — conventions invented for this widget. Tallmadge’s supplementary substitution alphabet was different; the 800-series and 998/999 markers are not transcribed from his key.

What the Book Gets Right

Atbash is a real Hebrew cipher used in the Old Testament. Leonardo da Vinci genuinely wrote in mirror script, though scholars believe this reflected left-handedness and personal habit rather than cryptographic intent. The novel offers fictional examples of pattern recognition and contextual guessing, both useful in some forms of historical cryptanalysis.

What the Book Gets Wrong

The Priory of Sion conspiracy is largely a 20th-century hoax; the “historical” documents Brown cites were forged in the 1960s by Pierre Plantard. The Fibonacci key usage described in the novel conflates several distinct cipher systems. Numerous art-historical details (Mona Lisa dimensions, Last Supper iconography) are anachronistic or fabricated. #### Why This Matters

DES made a complete modern block-cipher specification widely available as a US federal standard. Its public scrutiny helped establish academic cryptanalysis. Differential and linear cryptanalysis used DES as a major test case and influenced the design of later ciphers.

Historical Context

IBM developed DES from its earlier cipher research, with technical advice from NSA, and NBS standardised it in 1977. Biham and Shamir’s public differential cryptanalysis prompted renewed examination of its design. In 1994, IBM designer Don Coppersmith published S-box and permutation criteria that had been chosen to resist such attacks. His account establishes IBM’s earlier knowledge and design role; the S-boxes should not be attributed simply to NSA.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-gear"></use></svg>
```

How It Works

DES is a 16-round Feistel network. The 64-bit plaintext is split into two 32-bit halves L and R. Each round computes `L<sub>i+1</sub> = R<sub>i</sub>` and `R<sub>i+1</sub> = L<sub>i</sub> ⊕ F(R<sub>i</sub>, K<sub>i</sub>)`. The round function F expands R to 48 bits, XORs the round subkey, passes the result through eight 6-to-4-bit S-boxes (the heart of DES's non-linearity), and applies a final permutation. Sixteen rounds of this with sixteen subkeys derived from the 56-bit master key produce a reversible block cipher. Its 56-bit key is too small to withstand modern exhaustive search.

#### A differential through one S-box

XORing the same subkey into two inputs leaves their difference unchanged: `(x XOR k) XOR (x′ XOR k) = x XOR x′`. The nonlinear S-box then produces some output differences more often than others. For DES S1 and input difference `34`, enumerate all 64 possible six-bit inputs:

Selected S1 difference-distribution entries; differences are hexadecimal

| Output difference | Count out of 64 | Probability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 02 | 16 | 1/4 |
| 04 | 2 | 1/32 |
| 00 | 0 | 0 |
| 09 | 0 | 0 |

Now suppose a teaching example exposes the inputs *before* the subkey XOR and the S1 output difference. Keep each six-bit candidate k for which `S1(x XOR k) XOR S1(x′ XOR k)` matches that observation. All values below are hexadecimal:

Filtering the 64 possible S1 subkeys

| Inputs | Output XOR | Surviving subkeys |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 01, 35 | 0D | 07, 11, 17, 1D, 23, 25, 29, 33 |
| 21, 15 | 03 | 00, 14, 17, 20, 23, 34 |

The intersection is **`{17, 23}`**, so these observations do not identify a unique subkey. Both pairs use input difference `34`; a suitable observation with a different difference is needed to distinguish these survivors. This component example assumes exposed intermediate information, not ordinary intercepted DES ciphertexts or full-DES key recovery.

Eli Biham and Adi Shamir, *Differential Cryptanalysis of the Data Encryption Standard* (1993), §3.2, Examples 3.4–3.7. Numbered pp. 15–22, especially pp. 18–20, refer to the authors’ repaginated 7 December 2009 recompilation.

How It Works

Each pad page carries lines of random key letters, used once and destroyed. The DIANA rule is that plaintext, key, and cipher letters of every position sum to 25 modulo 26 (with A=0 … Z=25): **C = 25 − P − K (mod 26)**. That makes the system doubly reciprocal — the same Tri-Graph table both encrypts and decrypts, and any two letters of the trigram give the third — so an operator who memorized the 126 trigram combinations could work without the card at all.

```text
Worked arithmetic example (not evidence of a particular deployment):

Pad key:    GORWY WETFR COYET
Plaintext:  ATTAC KATDA WNXYZ
Ciphertext: TSPDZ TVNRI BYEXH

First letter: A(0) with key G(6) → 25−0−6 = 19 = T
Decryption is the same lookup: T with key G → 25−19−6 = 0 = A
```

ℹ️

**About this demo:** The demo implements the authentic DIANA rule (C = 25 − P − K mod 26) but repeats your short keyword as the key so you can experiment — a real DIANA pad used truly random, never-repeated key letters, which is what made it unbreakable.


Why Pads Fail in Practice

Pad reuse / loss / capture

Risk depends on which key material is compromised

The trigraph lookup card is public conversion information, not the secret pad. Capturing that card alone does not decrypt messages. Capturing the corresponding pad exposes the text it protects; reusing pad letters reveals relations between messages, but does not guarantee an immediate complete decipherment. Key generation, distribution, protection and destruction all matter. Perfect secrecy also supplies no message authentication.


Relationship to Modern Crypto

For a fixed-length message, independently uniform secret pad letters make each ciphertext equally likely regardless of that message. Reusing the pad invalidates this one-time guarantee. Modern symmetric encryption instead aims at computational security under stated assumptions and usage limits. TLS is a protocol, not a single symmetric cipher, and neither approach hides all metadata automatically.

Secure (Modern)](<https://ciphermuseum.com/museum-map.html#trust-labels>)

Key agreement over a public channel: computational assumptions protect against passive eavesdropping; authentication addresses active impersonation.

OriginWhitfield Diffie & Martin Hellman, ‘New Directions in Cryptography' (1976); Ralph Merkle’s related public-key research

Year1976

TypePublic-key key-agreement protocol — **not a cipher** (does not encrypt data)

StatusWidely used; finite-field DH and elliptic-curve variants such as X25519 have different parameter choices

Modern RoleContributes shared secrets to TLS, SSH, Signal and WireGuard; some protocols also combine post-quantum mechanisms

#### Why This Matters

Diffie–Hellman showed how two parties can agree on a secret over a public channel without first transporting that secret. The unauthenticated exchange protects against a passive eavesdropper under its computational assumptions. A secure protocol must also authenticate the participants or their exchanged values to resist an active intermediary.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-scroll"></use></svg>
```

Historical Context

Diffie and Hellman published ‘New Directions in Cryptography' in *IEEE Transactions on Information Theory*, November 1976. The paper proposed both public-key encryption (as a goal) and the key-exchange protocol (as a working example). Three British researchers at GCHQ — James Ellis, Clifford Cocks, and Malcolm Williamson — developed classified public-key ideas during 1970–1974 but classification kept their work secret until 1997. The 1976 paper directly inspired Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman to find RSA the following year.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-gear"></use></svg>
```

How It Works

Both parties agree on a large prime *p* and a generator *g*. Alice picks a secret integer *a* and sends *g<sup>a</sup> mod p*. Bob picks a secret integer *b* and sends *g<sup>b</sup> mod p*. Each computes the shared value *K = g<sup>ab</sup> mod p* — Alice raises Bob's value to her secret, Bob raises Alice's value to his. An eavesdropper sees *p*, *g*, *A = g<sup>a</sup>* and *B = g<sup>b</sup>*. Recovering an exponent *a* or *b* is a **discrete logarithm problem (DLP)**. Computing only the shared value *K* is the **computational Diffie–Hellman problem (CDH)**. A DLP solver gives a CDH solver: recover *a*, then compute *B<sup>a</sup>*. The converse is not established for arbitrary groups. See [Reduction & Proof](<https://ciphermuseum.com/hall-of-foundations/reduction.html>) for why the direction of an implication matters.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-scroll"></use></svg>
```

One direction, with toy numbers

With *p* = 23, *g* = 5, *A* = 8 and *B* = 19, a discrete-log solver finds *a* = 6 because 5<sup>6</sup> mod 23 = 8. It can then compute the shared value 19<sup>6</sup> mod 23 = 2. These tiny parameters are for arithmetic only.

Source: Dan Boneh and Victor Shoup, *A Graduate Course in Applied Cryptography*, version 0.6 (January 2023), §10.4.2. Learning whether the value looks random is a further security question, distinct from merely computing it.

#### Which oracle makes the reverse reduction possible?

A DLP solver gives a CDH solver. The reverse direction needs additional, group-specific conditions. A **full two-input DH oracle** accepts two freely chosen points `[a]G, [b]G` and returns `[ab]G`, multiplying hidden scalars. Ordinary point addition gives `[a+b]G`. A fixed-secret service that only returns `[s]P` is a different capability and does not supply the paper's efficient method.

Reported P256 experiment with a simulated full DH oracle

| Offline work | Online work | Required access |
| --- | --- | --- |
| About 3.0 TB auxiliary codebook; about one week on a dual AMD EPYC 7742 | 23,656 simulated full-oracle calls in 32 seconds | The experiment knew discrete logs to simulate the full oracle; the authors report no real-world instantiation |

These results do not establish that P256 was broken in 32 seconds. Other curves' figures are partly estimates, and the largest codebooks are unrealizable. The oracle, simulation status, offline cost and online time are inseparable parts of the result. Source: [Dlog is Practically as Hard (or Easy) as DH](<https://tches.iacr.org/index.php/TCHES/article/view/11161>), pp. 2–3, 7, 12–16.

#### From a shared value to keys for different purposes

HKDF-SHA-256 first **Extracts** a pseudorandom key (PRK) from input key material (IKM): `PRK = HMAC-SHA-256(salt, IKM)`. It then **Expands** that PRK with an `info` byte string and requested output length. A protocol can use different labels for its two traffic directions.

Reproducible fixture: IKM is the 32 bytes `00 01 … 1f` (hexadecimal), salt is 32 zero bytes, and labels below are UTF-8. These published bytes have no secrecy; they are separate from the tiny DH arithmetic above. Extract gives:

`46bd320605c5a6b6163ab70bc6345b92a5f908e79fe58979c23ebb47d1a5e307`

Changing the direction label changes this example's output. Salt and info need not be secret. HKDF does not create new input entropy or make a guessable password or tiny DH group secure; it is not a password-stretching function. Authentication and group validation remain necessary.

Security & Cryptanalysis

Resistance to recovering the shared value rests on the computational Diffie–Hellman assumption in the selected group; discrete-log hardness alone does not establish that assumption. Parameter size, subgroup choice and public-key validation all matter; a bit length alone does not establish security. The 2015 Logjam work demonstrated a downgrade to export-grade 512-bit finite-field DH and studied the benefits of precomputation for widely shared groups. Suitable group sizes and validation matter; moving to an elliptic curve also requires a specified secure construction. DH is vulnerable to *active* man-in-the-middle attacks because the exchanged values are unauthenticated — protocols that need protection from active attackers must combine DH with authentication (for example signatures, certificates or a suitable pre-shared-key protocol). A human password requires a properly designed password-authenticated key exchange, not an improvised password check.

Two input-validation failures are often merged and should not be. Forcing a peer’s shared secret into a small subgroup compromises that session and can leak a few bits; recovering a server’s *static* private exponent additionally requires that one exponent be reused across many such queries. A 2016 measurement study found that none of more than twenty surveyed libraries validated subgroup orders by default before January 2016, yet feasible full key recovery was confined to particular applications. The elliptic-curve counterpart — altering only the *y* coordinate of a peer’s public point — broke Bluetooth pairing in 2018. Both are laid out with their access and assumptions in [what an attack result actually shows](<https://ciphermuseum.com/lab/modern-attack-evidence.html#small-subgroup>).

#### Dilly Knox

1884 – 1943

Alfred Dillwyn Knox — universally 'Dilly' — was a classical scholar who became a leading British cryptanalyst. He deciphered German naval codes in WWI from Room 40, solved the commercial Enigma in the 1930s, and at Bletchley Park broke the Abwehr (German intelligence) Enigma variant — the system that protected Germany's spy networks across occupied Europe. A notoriously eccentric figure who often worked from his bathtub, Knox died of cancer in February 1943, before the end of the war.

##### Why This Person Matters

Knox worked on German naval systems in Room 40 during the First World War and later on commercial and military Enigma variants. His team, including Mavis Batey and Margaret Rock, achieved important Abwehr Enigma breaks. Those contributions should be distinguished from Marian Rejewski’s reconstruction of German Army Enigma wiring in Poland in 1932.

Commercial Enigma machines could be purchased and examined. Knox developed attacks on related systems, including a method known as rodding. British, Polish, and French work supplied different parts of the later Allied effort; no single prewar researcher was the predecessor of every success.

Historical Context

This page reconstructs one edition: [*Vorläufige Schlüsselanleitung zum Doppelkastenschlüssel*, Ausgabe Dezember 1941](<https://cryptocellar.org/wmc/schluesselanleitung-dk-1941.pdf>). The German armed forces used a two-box digraph cipher in WWII; its arrangement and repeated substitution distinguish it from the generic [Two-Square exhibit](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/two-square.html>).

Related procedures changed over time. The 1940 instructions, the later *Truppenschlüssel* and *Nachrichtenschlüssel* are not interchangeable names for this implementation. In particular, the Truppenschlüssel’s single-stage substitution must not be substituted for this edition’s two stages. RS44 began replacing Double Playfair during 1944.

Exact December 1941 Procedure

- Prepare letters; J is represented by II (two letters), as §4 requires. If the length is odd, append an operator-selected letter other than X. This demo chooses Z by default and never removes it automatically.

- Write each full 34-letter block as two rows of 17. Divide a final shorter, even block equally between two shorter rows; do not pad it to 17 columns.

- Form pairs vertically: top-row letter first, bottom-row letter second. Locate the first in square A and the second in square B.

- If the positions share a row, replace each by its right neighbour, wrapping at the edge, and output the B replacement first. Otherwise take the rectangle corners: first output from B at A’s row and B’s column, second from A at B’s row and A’s column.

- Apply that same rule again to the intermediate pair, still locating its first letter in A and its second in B. The second result is ciphertext.

For decryption, locate the ciphertext pair’s first letter in B and second in A. Apply the inverse rule twice: same-row moves are leftward, and rectangle corners cross back. Rebuild the two plaintext rows and read the top row before the bottom. Adjacent repeated letters are not split by a Playfair-style X.

#### Why This Matters

Encryption protects confidentiality. A digital signature lets a verifier check a message against a public key. Certificates, authorization rules and context connect that key to a domain, person or permitted action. ECDSA is used for many certificates, software signatures and cryptocurrency transactions, although those ecosystems also use other signature schemes.

It is also the museum's clearest case of the difference between a mathematical signature construction and implementation failures. Nonce mistakes are a particularly revealing example; secure implementation also requires careful arithmetic, validation and side-channel protection.

Historical Context

Elliptic curves entered cryptography in 1985, proposed independently by Neal Koblitz and Victor Miller. The appeal was efficiency: the discrete logarithm problem seemed much harder on a curve than in the integers modulo a prime, so a 256-bit curve key offered roughly the security of a 3072-bit RSA key. Scott Vanstone adapted the existing Digital Signature Algorithm to curves in 1992, and ECDSA was standardised as ANSI X9.62 in 1999. Bitcoin adopted it in 2009, which put the algorithm in front of a far larger and more adversarial audience than the standards bodies had imagined.

How It Works

Fix a curve, a base point *G* on it, and the number *n* of points *G* generates. A private key is a number *d*; the matching public key is the point *Q* = *dG*. Recovering *d* from *Q* means solving the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem, for which no practical classical attack is known on properly chosen large groups.

To sign a message hash *h*, pick a one-time random number *k* — the **nonce** — and compute:

R = kG · r = R<sub>x</sub> mod n · s = k<sup>−1</sup>(h + rd) mod n

The signature is the pair (*r*, *s*). A verifier who has only *Q*, *h* and the signature recomputes a point from them and reduces its x-coordinate modulo *n* and compares the result with *r*. The private key never appears in the check — that asymmetry is the whole point.

The nonce that sank the PlayStation 3

The nonce *k* must be secret. Reusing it with **distinct message-hash values modulo *n*** is dangerous: the two signature equations then share an unknown. Subtract them and *k* falls out; substitute back and the private key follows. No lattice, no supercomputer — secondary-school algebra:

k = (h₁ − h₂) / (s₁ − s₂) then d = (s₁k − h₁) / r

All arithmetic in these recovery formulas is modulo *n*; division means multiplication by a modular inverse.

In December 2010 fail0verflow demonstrated that reused ECDSA nonces exposed PlayStation 3 signing keys. The example shows why secrecy of the long-term key is not enough: the per-signature nonce must be generated and handled correctly. Deterministic nonce generation, as specified by RFC 6979, is one established way to avoid relying on fresh randomness for each signature; it does not remove other implementation risks.

Security & Cryptanalysis

No practical classical attack is known against properly parameterised ECDSA with suitable curves and hashes. Implementations can fail through nonce reuse, weak randomness, faults, or side-channel leakage. **Partial nonce leakage can enable key recovery**, but success depends on the amount and structure of leakage, measurement noise and the number of signatures available; it is not an unconditional result for any leaked bit.

**Deterministic ECDSA** derives the nonce from the private key and message hash, removing dependence on fresh signing randomness. With the same inputs it produces the same signature. It does **not guarantee collision-free nonces** for different messages, and it does not remove side-channel or fault risks. Correct randomised ECDSA is also possible; secure key generation still needs suitable randomness.

Source: [RFC 6979 (August 2013), §§1, 3.2 and 4](<https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6979.html#section-4>), an Informational RFC. Its deterministic nonce procedure retains implementation-security requirements. This exhibit uses visitor-selected toy nonces and does not implement RFC 6979.

Like all discrete-logarithm cryptography, ECDSA falls to a sufficiently large quantum computer running Shor's algorithm. [Resource estimates distinguish logical/physical qubits and modeled runtime](<https://ciphermuseum.com/lab/modern-attack-evidence.html#quantum-resources>); RSA figures do not automatically transfer to elliptic-curve targets. ML-DSA is one standardized post-quantum signature option and uses module-lattice assumptions — the [Learning With Errors](<https://ciphermuseum.com/hall-of-foundations/learning-with-errors.html>) exhibit introduces one relevant family of assumptions, not a proof of the complete signature scheme.

Where You Use It Today

ECDSA is one signature option in TLS, cryptocurrency protocols and software verification. TLS also supports other signature schemes, and a resumed or PSK handshake need not send a fresh certificate signature. Bitcoin uses both ECDSA and, for Taproot, BIP 340 Schnorr signatures. A browser’s connection indicator does not identify the signature algorithm.

At a glance

**Private key:** a number *d*.

**Public key:** the point *Q* = *dG*.

**Signature:** a pair (*r*, *s*).

**Hard problem:** find *d* given *Q* — the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm.

**Fatal mistake:** reusing the nonce *k* for distinct message-hash values modulo *n*. Two valid signatures then expose the private key.

The mathematics is in the [Hall of Foundations](<https://ciphermuseum.com/hall-of-foundations/elliptic-curves-real.html>): the group law that makes *dG* meaningful, and the finite field it runs in.

Historical Context

Egyptian scribes had two parallel writing systems by 1900 BCE: monumental hieroglyphs for inscriptions and the cursive hieratic for everyday documents. The Khnumhotep tomb belongs to the monumental tradition, where elaboration was prized. Egyptologists debate whether the unusual variants were meant to obscure meaning from casual readers, to flatter the gods through visual richness, or to display the scribe's erudition. All three motives — secrecy, sanctity, and craft — recur throughout cipher history.
