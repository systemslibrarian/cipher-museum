##### Why This Matters

In German musical notation, B♭ is written 'B' and B♮ is written 'H'. The four-note sequence B♭–A–C–B♮ therefore literally *spells* the surname BACH. Johann Sebastian Bach used this motif as an embedded signature in several works, most famously as the third subject of the unfinished final fugue (Contrapunctus XIV) of *The Art of Fugue*, BWV 1080. The surviving fugue breaks off unfinished. That physical ending does not establish that Bach died while writing it, and the BACH subject appears before the break.

Historical Context

The German naming convention treats H as the 'natural' B and B as the 'flat' B, a leftover of medieval mode notation. This idiosyncrasy gave German composers a unique alphabet of pitches: every musical note name from A through H is also a letter. Later composers including Schumann, Liszt, Brahms, Reger, Schoenberg, Webern, and Shostakovich used or paid tribute to the BACH motif. Shostakovich extended the idea to spell his own initials: D-S-C-H = D – E♭ – C – B♮ (since 'Es' = E♭ and 'H' = B♮ in German), the four notes that open his Eighth String Quartet and haunt the Tenth Symphony.

Technical Notes

As a cipher this is a one-to-one substitution between the eight German letter names A–H and their pitch classes (B and H distinguish B-flat and B-natural). Its 'plaintext space' is therefore tiny — only words spelled with A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H can be encoded. It is closer to a *signature* than to encryption. But it demonstrates a principle that recurs across cipher culture: a structured medium can carry an encoding. A public mapping alone does not provide confidentiality.

Modern Echo

Modern composers continue the tradition: Alban Berg's *Lyric Suite* embeds the initials of his secret love Hanna Fuchs-Robettin; Olivier Messiaen developed a 'communicable language' that spelled prayers in French phonetic note-equivalents; computer-music practitioners encode entire ASCII strings into MIDI. The BACH motif illustrates a musical signature. Calling it a cryptogram does not make it secret-key encryption or establish it as the first musical use of encoded meaning.

```javascript
      };
```

#### Why This Matters

Francis Bacon’s cipher — devised by 1605 and fully described in his 1623 *De Augmentis* — was one of the earliest documented uses of binary encoding — representing each letter as a 5-bit sequence over two centuries before Boolean algebra and three centuries before digital computing.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-scroll"></use></svg>
```

Historical Context

Sir Francis Bacon described his cipher in *De Augmentis Scientiarum* (1623). Each letter of the alphabet is represented by a 5-bit binary sequence using only two symbols (A and B). Bacon's original application was steganography: hide a secret message within an innocuous text by printing the covering text’s letters in two distinguishable typefaces — for example, roman and italic in a teaching illustration. Bacon’s proposal used two alphabets with subtler differences.

Bacon’s two-symbol alphabet is an early documented example of binary representation. Its historical interest does not establish a direct line of invention to Boolean algebra or electronic computing.

How It Works

```text
Bacon's binary alphabet:
A = AAAAA    N = ABBAA
B = AAAAB    O = ABBAB
C = AAABA    P = ABBBA
D = AAABB    Q = ABBBB
E = AABAA    R = BAAAA
...
Z = BBAAA

Example: HI = AABBB ABAAA
(each letter = 5 A/B symbols)

Following the common modern convention (Bacon's own alphabet had 24 letters, merging U/V as well), I and J share
the same code ABAAA (a 25-letter alphabet).

Steganographic use:
Normal text: AABBB ABAAA
Hidden in cover text by using two fonts:
normal=A, italic=B across 10 characters
```

How It Was Broken

A/B Distribution Analysis

Complexity: Trivial (as cipher) · Moderate (as steganography)

As a pure cipher, Bacon is trivially broken — it's a fixed binary encoding of the alphabet. As steganography in printed text, detection requires identifying two typefaces, which is usually obvious with magnification. Modern steganography detection uses statistical analysis of pixel distributions or bit-level patterns.

Historical Context

Étienne Bazeries was one of the most famous cryptanalysts of his era — he reconstructed a Rossignol-era nomenclator in correspondence from about two centuries earlier. Ironically, he also designed his own cipher and spent years trying to convince the French military to adopt it, without success.

Bazeries proposed a 20-disk cylinder independently of Jefferson’s earlier 36-disk description. The later U.S. M-94 belongs to a related but separately developed cylinder tradition; none of these devices is the two-square hand procedure described below.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

How It Works

**ACA hand-cipher convention:** choose a number and spell it out to key a 5×5 ciphertext square, merging I/J. The plaintext square has its alphabet written down columns. Reverse groups of plaintext according to successive key digits, then substitute between corresponding square cells.

For example, key 3752 uses successive group lengths 3, 7, 5 and 2. This is group reversal, not columnar transposition.

**Browser model boundary:** the widget above instead accepts 2–99, builds a 26-letter keyword substitution from the English number name, and reverses fixed-size groups. The size is min(6, max(2, (key mod 7)+2)); key 42 therefore uses pairs. It does not implement the ACA two-square rule or the mechanical cylinder. Its outputs are model exercises, not historical test vectors.

#### Why This Matters

Two dates run through this exhibit and they are not interchangeable. **1885** is when the ciphers demonstrably enter the record: James B. Ward published a pamphlet in Lynchburg, Virginia, titled *The Beale Papers*, containing three numerical ciphertexts. **The 1820s are only what that pamphlet claims** — that a man named Thomas Jefferson Beale buried a fortune in gold, silver and jewels near Buford's Tavern in Bedford County in 1819 and 1821, and left the box of papers with innkeeper Robert Morriss in 1822. No ciphertext, letter or document from the Beale story is attested anywhere before 1885.

The pamphlet's story assigns each cipher a job: cipher 1 names the location, cipher 2 itemises the contents, cipher 3 names the heirs. Its anonymous narrator, who says he received the papers from Morriss and worked on them for some twenty years, solved cipher 2 by trying the Declaration of Independence as the key text and read out a precise inventory of buried treasure. Ciphers 1 and 3 have no generally accepted decipherment.

Historical Context

The treasure story’s authenticity is disputed. The surviving 1885 publication is evidence that the story and numerical texts were circulated then; it does not independently authenticate the claimed earlier events. A readable construction for cipher 2 can be created deliberately, so its decoding does not settle whether treasure existed or whether ciphers 1 and 3 encode meaningful messages. Statistical arguments about those sequences have also produced conflicting interpretations, as the sources below explain.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

How It Works

A book cipher converts each plaintext letter to the position of a word in a reference text whose first letter matches.

```text
Plaintext: G O L D
           ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓
Declaration words: 48=god, 12=one, 42=laws, 15=dissolve
Ciphertext: 48 12 42 15
```

To decrypt, you need the exact same edition of the reference text the encoder used. An insertion or omission can shift later word numbers; a wrong lookup can also corrupt only the affected letter.

How It Was Broken

Beale 2: Right Book Found

Complexity: Lucky guess

The anonymous narrator of the 1885 pamphlet supplies a Declaration-of-Independence decoding of the second sequence. That demonstrates a readable construction for B2, not the historical truth of the treasure claim or that B1 and B3 must have meaningful plaintexts and missing books.

Beale 1 & 3: Still Open

Status: No generally accepted decipherment

B1 and B3 have no generally accepted decipherment. Hammer and Gillogly should not be presented as reaching the same conclusion: Kahn reports that Hammer regarded his tests as support for a genuine cipher, whereas Gillogly’s 1980 paper argues for a hoax interpretation. Alphabetical runs produced by applying the Declaration key to B1 are evidence to examine under an explicit model, not proof of either a treasure or a hoax.

#### Why This Matters

The Beaufort cipher is a Vigenère variant with an elegant reciprocal property: the same operation both encrypts and decrypts. Its reciprocal mapping simplifies the operator’s procedure while retaining the vulnerabilities of a repeating key.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

Historical Context

Sir Francis Beaufort — famous for the Beaufort wind scale — devised a variant of the Vigenère cipher with an elegant property: the same operation encrypts and decrypts. Where Vigenère adds key to plaintext modulo 26, Beaufort subtracts plaintext from key. Because of this reciprocal property, you never need to switch between an encryption and decryption mode.

David Kahn describes a cipher card published under Beaufort’s name in 1857, after his death. His naval rank does not by itself establish Royal Navy operational adoption. Its weakness is identical to Vigenère's: the repeating key creates detectable periodicity.

How It Works

The Beaufort cipher uses a tabula recta like Vigenère but with a different lookup direction:

```text
Vigenère: C = (P + K) mod 26
Beaufort: C = (K - P + 26) mod 26

Key:    S  E  C  R  E  T
Plain:  H  E  L  L  O  W
Cipher: L  A  R  G  Q  X
```

Because K − (K − P) = P modulo 26: Decrypt(C, K) = Encrypt(C, K). This simplifies the operation; it does not remove the need to distribute and protect the key.

How It Was Broken

Kasiski Examination

Complexity: Moderate

The Beaufort cipher shares Vigenère's fatal flaw: the repeating key. Identical plaintext segments that align with identical key segments produce identical ciphertext. Repetition distances and index-of-coincidence tests can suggest a period. Language scoring can then rank key-letter candidates; short texts and accidental repetitions can mislead.

#### Arne Beurling

1905 – 1986

Arne Beurling was a Swedish mathematician. In 1940 he reconstructed essential features of the Siemens T52 from intercepted traffic. The [FRA’s historical account](<https://fra.se/omfra/frashistoria/gskrivarenocharnebeurling.4.55af049f184e92956c42a59.html>) credits his principal breakthrough to about two weeks, with assistance from Hans Rudberg and Bertil Nyman.

##### Why This Person Matters

Reconstructing an unknown cipher machine from its traffic is an exceptional analytic achievement. It is different from recovering every later message key. Daily-key work and practical decryption required other analysts and equipment; treating the whole operation as a solitary, permanent solution hides those contributions.

The Geheimschreiber Problem

The T52 enciphered five-bit teleprinter signals using ten pinwheels to control bit mixing and permutation. These were not Enigma-style wired alphabet rotors. Traffic sent through Sweden provided intercepted material for the attack; the wheel mechanism and operating procedure both mattered.

The Two Weeks

The interception operation belonged to the Swedish Defence Staff’s cryptographic service; FRA became an independent agency in 1942. After the structural breakthrough, teams still had to solve daily settings. Beurling and engineer Vigo Lindstein developed equipment for decryption. The initial success did not guarantee uninterrupted access to every later variant or all wartime traffic.

Historical Context

In 1994 Israeli mathematicians Doron Witztum, Eliyahu Rips, and Yoav Rosenberg published a paper in *Statistical Science* claiming to find statistically significant equidistant letter sequences (ELS) in the Hebrew text of Genesis. They claimed that names of famous rabbis and associated dates occurred unusually close together under their chosen ELS proximity measure.

The paper caused a sensation. In 1997 journalist Michael Drosnin published *The Bible Code*, extending the method to predict modern events — the assassination of Yitzhak Rabin (cited after the fact), the Gulf War, and others. Drosnin's challenge: "When my critics find a message about the assassination of a prime minister encrypted in *Moby-Dick*, I'll believe them."

Brendan McKay published *Moby-Dick* assassination-name examples in response to Drosnin’s popular challenge. This was distinct from the 1999 statistical paper by McKay, Bar-Natan, Bar-Hillel and Kalai, which examined the Genesis experiment’s data and design choices and reported unsuccessful new tests. Neither finding means that every text has the same matches.

How ELS Works

Take any text. Remove all spaces, punctuation, and numbers, keeping only letters. This gives you a letter string. Now pick a target word and a **skip value** *d*. Starting at position *p*, read every *d*-th letter: positions *p*, *p+d*, *p+2d*, and so on. If the result spells your target word, you have found an ELS at (start=*p*, skip=*d*).

```text
Text = ABCDEFGHI, target = ADG
Zero-based start 0, positive skip 3: positions 0, 3, 6.

For length N, target length m, and positive skips 1…D:
valid start/skip pairs = Σ max(N − (m−1)d, 0).

N = 100,000, m = 4, D = 1,000:
98,498,500 valid pairs (not 100,000,000).
Under independent uniformly distributed A–Z letters,
expected hits = 98,498,500 / 26⁴ ≈ 215.54.
```

The expectation above uses an **independent uniform-letter model**, not real prose. With independently sampled nonuniform letters, multiply the number of valid pairs by the product of the target letters’ probabilities. Overlapping searches need not be independent for this expectation, but a significance test needs an appropriate distribution and must account for other searched words and settings.

How the Claim Was Refuted

Statistical Replication Test (McKay et al., 1999)

Method: Examine experimental flexibility and attempt independent tests

The 1999 paper examines choices in the Genesis experiment. Its §6 demonstrates how changing permissible rabbinical names and spellings can produce a strong-looking result in a Hebrew *War and Peace* passage while weakening the Genesis result. It is a demonstration of selection flexibility, not an identical search using an unchanged list in both books. The separate *Moby-Dick* examples address Drosnin’s popular interpretation.

The Multiplicity Problem (searching for significance)

Method: Combinatorial argument

Trying more names, spellings, skips, directions, and texts creates more opportunities for matches. Hits are not certain for every target, and no single search-space count applies without specifying those choices. Exploratory discoveries need independent confirmation or a statistical method that accounts for the selection process.

What This Teaches Modern Cryptography

| Mistake in the Bible Code claim | Modern cryptographic parallel |
| --- | --- |
|  Searching billions of (start, skip) pairs post-hoc and reporting only the hits |  **Statistical significance in security audits:** under valid null hypotheses, 1,000 tests at a 0.05 threshold have an expected 50 false positives; the observed count need not be exactly 50 |
|  Treating a pattern found in scripture as non-accidental without comparing to a random baseline |  **Distinguisher attacks:** a valid linear or differential distinguisher requires showing the bias in the cipher is *greater than expected from a random permutation*, not merely that a bias exists |
|  Unaccounted flexibility in selecting names, spellings and tests can change reported significance |  **Chosen-input bias:** choosing a dataset after inspecting results can invalidate a test’s stated significance |
|  Short texts and flexible interpretation can permit competing readings |  State the cipher model and constraints before judging a proposed decipherment; a plausible phrase alone does not identify the original message |
|  Publication and independent examination can reach different conclusions |  **Security proofs require adversarial review:** a published claim still needs independent examination of its assumptions, evidence, and reproducibility; publication alone does not settle it |

The Numbers

For an illustrative text of 78,000 letters, a five-letter target, and positive skips 1–1,000, there are 75,998,000 valid start/skip pairs. Compare two explicitly idealized alphabets:

75,998,000 start × skip pairs searched

1 in 26⁵ = 1 in 11.9M chance per pair

~6.40 expected hits: independent uniform A–Z model

~14.75 expected hits: independent uniform 22-letter model (N / 22⁵)

These expectations are for artificial independent-letter models. Hebrew has 22 base letters, not 26; its real letter frequencies and textual structure are not uniform. A number of hits alone cannot establish significance, and a 78,000-letter example should not be mislabeled as the complete Torah.

Historical Context

Felix Delastelle was a French amateur cryptographer who invented several remarkable ciphers in the late 19th century. The Bifid (from Latin *bifidus*, “split in two,” from *findere*, “to split”) is widely considered his masterpiece. It combines a Polybius square with a transposition technique in a way that creates genuine interdependence between letter positions — a property Shannon would later call diffusion.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-gear"></use></svg>
```

How It Works

Using a 5×5 Polybius square with a keyword:

- Convert each plaintext letter to its (row, col) coordinates

- Write all row numbers in a row, then all column numbers beneath

- Read pairs from the combined sequence

- Convert each pair back to a letter using the square

```text
Key square (keyword SECRET):
  S E C R T
  A B D F G
  H I K L M
  N O P Q U
  V W X Y Z

HELLO → H(2,0) E(0,1) L(2,3) L(2,3) O(3,1)
Rows:    2  0  2  2  3
Cols:    0  1  3  3  1
Combined: 2 0 2 2 3 | 0 1 3 3 1
Pairs: (2,0)(2,2)(3,0)(1,3)(3,1)
Cipher: HKNFO
```

Complexity: Moderate-Hard

Bifid mixes coordinate information within a chosen period. Some variants use fixed-size groups; this demo uses its documented grouping rule. Computer attacks can search candidate squares using language scores, but success depends on period, text length, key conventions, and search effort. No fixed few-hundred-character guarantee applies universally.

#### Bill Tutte

1917 – 2002

William Thomas Tutte was a Cambridge chemistry graduate and research student when he was recruited to Bletchley Park in 1941. Assigned to the Research Section working on "Fish" — the German teleprinter cipher systems — he was given a 4,000-character depth: two messages that an operator had transmitted in the same Lorenz SZ40 settings, with changes to the wording and abbreviations. John Tiltman first read the depth by hand: the XOR of the two ciphertexts is the XOR of the two plaintexts, which let him separate both messages and so recover the raw keystream. Tutte was then given that key to analyse. By analysing patterns in this recovered keystream, Tutte and colleagues in the Research Section inferred the machine’s structure by early 1942: the wheel groups, their lengths and their interaction. He did this entirely on paper, never having seen a Lorenz machine. His analysis was the foundation for the Tunny-breaking algorithms developed at Bletchley, which in turn drove the construction of the Robinson machines and ultimately Colossus. His work has been described by post-war analysts as "one of the greatest intellectual feats of World War II."

##### Why This Person Matters

Bill Tutte's Lorenz analysis is the purest example of what cryptanalysis at its highest level actually is: the reconstruction of an unknown physical system from its outputs alone. Unlike Enigma, where the machine's general design was known, the Lorenz SZ40 was entirely unknown to British intelligence when Tutte was given the key material Tiltman had recovered. The structure emerged through mathematical analysis. The practical consequence was Colossus — the machine designed by Tommy Flowers and programmed by a team to automate Tutte's statistical attacks — which became the world's first operational programmable digital electronic computer. The work connected cryptanalysis with an important early application of programmable electronics. Wartime secrecy long kept this contribution from wider public recognition. His postwar mathematical career was equally distinguished: he made major contributions to graph theory, matroid theory, and combinatorics. He holds the rare distinction of being a world-historical figure in two separate fields.

The Lorenz SZ40

The Lorenz SZ40 (Schlüsselzusatz — "cipher attachment") was a teleprinter cipher machine used for Hitler's highest-level communications — the "Führer's telegraph." Unlike Enigma (a letter substitution device), Lorenz worked on the 5-bit Baudot teleprinter code and combined the plaintext with a pseudorandom keystream generated by twelve rotating pin wheels. The keystream was the XOR of contributions from two groups of wheels (the Chi wheels and the Psi wheels), with two motor wheels controlling the irregular stepping of the Psi group. The complexity was far greater than Enigma: the number of possible wheel positions exceeded 10 to the 19th power. The cipher was used by German High Command, Army Group commanders, and directly by Hitler for strategic communications throughout the war.

The Depth and the Reconstruction

On August 30, 1941, a German operator sent a 4,000-character message to another station. The receiving station asked for a repeat. The operator retransmitted — using the same Lorenz wheel settings, but with wording and abbreviations changed. This pair of ciphertexts produced with a reused keystream was intercepted by the British "Y stations." Tutte examined repetitions and periodic structure in Tiltman’s recovered key. Working with the Research Section through early 1942, he inferred the machine’s wheel groups, sizes (41, 31, 29, 26, 23 in the Chi set; 43, 47, 51, 53, 59 for Psi; and 61, 37 for motor wheels) and their interaction. This was a tour de force of reverse engineering conducted entirely through mathematical inference.

Diagnosing an unseen machine, recovering wheel pin patterns and finding a message’s starting positions were separate tasks. Success at one supplied information for the next.

From Lorenz to Colossus

Tutte’s reconstruction and subsequent statistical methods supported both manual and machine-assisted Tunny cryptanalysis. The Newmanry’s Heath Robinson used two paper tapes and electronic counting; synchronising the tapes was difficult. Tommy Flowers and his team replaced the second tape’s wheel-pattern role with electronic generation in Colossus. Mark 1 entered operational use in February 1944. Colossus was a specialised programmable electronic digital machine, not a general-purpose stored-program computer. Manual analysis in the Testery remained essential alongside the machines.

The Organization

Bletchley Park housed several operational sections. Hut 6 attacked Army and Air Force Enigma; Hut 3 interpreted and reported the resulting intelligence. Hut 8 attacked Naval Enigma, with intelligence work in Hut 4. The Testery and Newmanry worked on Tunny, the British name for Lorenz traffic. The Bombe

The British Bombe was a specialized **electromechanical search machine**, designed by Alan Turing and improved through Gordon Welchman’s diagonal board and engineering work. It tested constraints derived from a crib and produced candidate settings for checking. Colossus and the Lorenz Break

Lorenz enciphered strategic teleprinter traffic. Tiltman’s recovered keystream made Tutte’s structural analysis possible. Tommy Flowers and his team built Colossus to accelerate statistical tests developed by the cryptanalytic organization; it did not simply decrypt arbitrary traffic automatically. Colossus Mark 1 worked at Dollis Hill in late 1943 and entered operational use at Bletchley in early 1944. See [The National Museum of Computing](<https://www.tnmoc.org/colossus>).

Secrecy and After

Staff were bound by official secrecy, and many accounts became public only decades later. F. W. Winterbotham’s *The Ultra Secret* (1974) brought the wartime intelligence story to a much wider readership, but was not a complete official history of every operation. Dilly Knox died during the war in 1943; others lived to see later recognition. The story involves analysts, engineers, intercept operators, translators and administrative staff, not only its best-known mathematicians.

### Turing–Welchman Bombe

A crib becomes a network of constraints; machinery searches for settings that survive.

Codebreaking machine; not an encryption system.

#### Why This Matters

The Bombe mechanised part of the attack on Enigma. Alan Turing developed the crib-based approach; Gordon Welchman’s diagonal board improved the search. British Tabulating Machine Company engineers built the machines. Operators and analysts remained essential.

#### How the Search Worked

A guessed plaintext passage, called a crib, was aligned with intercepted ciphertext. Its letter relationships formed a menu. The Bombe tested rotor arrangements against these constraints; contradictions eliminated candidates. A stop indicated a candidate to check, not a finished plaintext. Welchman’s diagonal board exploited the reciprocal connections of Enigma’s plugboard.

#### Read a Constraint

Suppose a proposed crib places A against ciphertext A. Enigma cannot encipher a letter as itself, so that alignment is impossible before a machine search begins. A different alignment may survive this first check and still fail the connected menu constraints. Surviving a filter is evidence to investigate, not proof of a correct key.

#### Historical Context and Limits

The first British Bombe, Victory, entered service in March 1940; the Turing–Welchman version followed in August. The earlier Polish bomba was an important predecessor with a different attack. This was an electromechanical codebreaking machine, not an encryption system or a general-purpose computer. It attacked Enigma, not Lorenz.

How It Works

Pick a book and an edition. To encode the word *RIVER*, scan the book until you find that word; record its location as `(page, line, word)` — say `(127, 14, 6)`. Send the triplet. Historical Context

Julius Caesar used this cipher to communicate with his generals during the Gallic Wars. Suetonius records it in *The Twelve Caesars* (c. 121 AD): *"If he had anything confidential to say, he wrote it in cipher, that is, by so changing the order of the letters of the alphabet, that not a word could be made out."*

Suetonius describes Caesar’s shift of three. His account of Augustus describes a different convention, including AA for Z rather than a modern wraparound alphabet. This exhibit’s fixed 26-letter model should not be mistaken for a facsimile of Roman correspondence.

💡

**ROT13** — the modern variant — applies a shift of 13. Because 13+13=26, ROT13 is its own inverse: applying it twice returns the original text. It's still used in online forums to hide spoilers.

How It Works

Each letter is replaced by the letter a fixed number of positions down the alphabet. The shift wraps around using modular arithmetic:

```text
Encrypt: C = (P + shift) mod 26
Decrypt: P = (C − shift + 26) mod 26

Example (shift = 3):
  A → D    N → Q
  B → E    O → R
  Z → C    (wrap-around)
```

**Encryption steps:**

- Convert each plaintext letter to a number (A=0, B=1 … Z=25)

- Add the shift value and take modulo 26

- Convert the result back to a letter

- Non-alphabetic characters (spaces, punctuation) pass through unchanged

How It Was Broken

Frequency Analysis

Al-Kindi described frequency analysis in the ninth century; no first break of Caesar’s messages is established here

Long English samples often have E among their most frequent letters, but individual messages may differ greatly. A Caesar shift preserves the observed letter counts. Treating the most common ciphertext letter as E proposes a key; other shifts and the resulting language must still be checked.

Brute Force

Complexity: Trivial · Keyspace: 25

The entire keyspace is just 25 shifts. There are only 25 nonidentity shifts to test. Recognizing the intended plaintext can still require context. This is the simplest form of exhaustive key search — a technique that scales to modern ciphers but requires vastly more computation.

⚠️

**Why it fails fundamentally:** there are only 25 nonidentity shifts on A–Z (26 including leaving the text unchanged). AES-256 has 2²⁵⁶ possible keys. The difference illustrates why a tiny keyspace is vulnerable, but keyspace size alone never proves a cipher secure.

What It Teaches Modern Cryptography

| Caesar Concept | Evolved Into |
| --- | --- |
| Letter substitution | AES combines nonlinear byte substitution with key addition and diffusion; a substitution table alone preserves frequencies |
| Shift = key | Symmetric key concept — both parties share a secret parameter |
| 25-key space | Generic search of a uniform AES-256 key takes about 2²⁵⁵ trials on average |
| Frequency leak | Diffusion in block-cipher design spreads input changes; this is not a property of every cryptographic construction |

🎓

**Shannon's confusion:** Claude Shannon (1949) defined "confusion" as making the relationship between key and ciphertext as complex as possible. Caesar’s relation `C = P + k (mod 26)` is easy to invert for each candidate key. “Zero confusion” is not a numerical security measurement. AES combines nonlinear substitution with other round operations; its strength cannot be attributed to an S-box alone.

#### Why This Matters

Cardano described using plaintext as key material, but the one-letter-primer algorithm below should not be attributed to him as an exact reconstruction. Holden’s [*The Mathematics of Secrets: Cryptography from Caesar Ciphers to Digital Encryption*](<https://ciphermuseum.com/further-reading.html#holden>) (2017), §5.3, pp. 157–159, distinguishes Cardano’s word-based scheme from Vigenère’s later use of a priming letter. This interactive isolates a simple autokey recurrence for teaching.

In this model, knowing one plaintext letter gives the next key letter and permits forward propagation. A primer of length k changes this to propagation every k positions; it does **not** eliminate the structural weakness. A sufficiently long correct crib can seed several such chains.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-gear"></use></svg>
```

How It Works

Pick a single seed letter `S`. The keystream is

```text
k₀ = S
kᵢ = pᵢ₋₁  for i ≥ 1
```

Encryption uses the standard Vigenère table: cᵢ = (pᵢ + kᵢ) mod 26. Decryption recovers p₀ from c₀ using S, then uses each just-recovered pᵢ as the key for cᵢ₊₁.

**Worked example with S = Q, plaintext MEET ME AT MIDNIGHT:**

```text
Plain : M E E T M E A T M I D N I G H T
Key   : Q M E E T M E A T M I D N I G H
Cipher: C Q I X F Q E T F U L Q V O N A
```

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-skull"></use></svg>
```

How It Was Broken

Probable-word cascade

Complexity: Easy with a crib

With a one-letter primer, a correct plaintext guess propagates one position at a time. Wrong guesses generate alternative chains that need evaluation against language or other evidence. Longer primers change the spacing of propagation rather than making plaintext feedback secure.

26 priming guesses

Complexity: Trivial on short messages

Try every possible seed letter. For each, run the cascade and look for English. The correct plaintext is among the 26 candidates; a short or unusual message may need context to distinguish it.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-lens"></use></svg>
```

What It Teaches Modern Cryptography

The lesson is that reusing message letters as raw key material does not supply independent secrecy. It should not be generalized into a rule that every data-dependent cryptographic construction is insecure: the precise construction and security definition matter.

CTR and ChaCha20 generate keystream from a secret key and nonce/counter state. Their security also depends on correct nonce use. The contrast is with this bare letter recurrence, not a claimed direct historical lineage or a universal ban on processing plaintext in authentication and encryption designs.

#### Why This Matters

Girolamo Cardano described a fixed concealment grille in *De Subtilitate* (1550). Openings in a sheet select parts of a message written underneath; the sender then fills the surrounding space with cover text. Openings can expose letters, syllables or words. The square, one-letter-per-cell layout below is a teaching model, not a requirement of the historical technique.

Variants and descendants of the grille remained in active espionage use into the twentieth century. The [**turning grille**](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/turning-grille.html>) — a square card rotated through four positions to fill an N×N grid completely — was used briefly by the German Army in late 1916–early 1917 for tactical traffic, and broken by French and British analysts within months.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-gear"></use></svg>
```

How It Works

In this teaching model, the grille is a square card of side *N* with holes punched at agreed-upon positions. Sender and receiver each have an identical card. To encrypt:

- Place the grille over a blank N×N grid.

- Write the secret letter-by-letter through the holes.

- Lift the grille; fill the remaining cells with plausible filler (a poem, a shopping list, a love letter).

To decrypt, the recipient places their grille over the received grid in the same orientation and reads the letters visible through the holes.

The demo on this page uses a key of the form `size:idx,idx,idx,…` — the grid side length followed by the zero-based positions of the holes. The default `5:0,3,7,12,19,21,24` places seven holes in a 5×5 grid.

ℹ️

**About this demo:** To guarantee that Decrypt exactly restores your original message, this demo reversibly escapes the letters X, Q, and Z (as ZA, ZB, ZC) before enciphering, so padding and filler X can never be confused with a real letter. Messages containing X, Q, or Z therefore produce slightly different ciphertext than a strictly classical Cardano grille would. Messages longer than one grille continue into additional grids rather than being truncated.

How It Was Broken

Capture the grille

Complexity: A search of the office

Copying the grille reveals its selection rule. Messages using that same layout and orientation can then be read if their carriers are available. A different grille, changed convention or separate encryption layer is a separate problem; capturing one card does not automatically expose every past and future message.

Style mismatch

Complexity: Easy with practice

Convincing filler text is hard. A trained censor reading thousands of letters notices stilted phrasing, awkward grammar, and the distinctive cadence of grille-fill — and flags suspicious correspondence for closer inspection.

Brute-force the holes

Complexity: Tractable for small grids

The default demo has seven holes in 25 cells: there are C(25,7) = 480,700 possible masks if only the hole count is known. This counts selections, not a guarantee of a unique readable extraction. Natural-language scoring can rank candidates; several short extractions may look plausible. A turning grille has different rotational constraints and needs its own count.

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-lens"></use></svg>
```

What It Teaches Modern Cryptography

The fixed grille illustrates **steganography**: concealing the existence or location of a message within a carrier. Modern image embedding and covert channels offer useful comparisons, but a direct historical line of descent is not established by the similarity.

Concealment and confidentiality are different goals. A plausible cover may escape notice, but this grille provides no encryption or authentication once the selected text is exposed. Separate encryption can protect content even if the hiding method is discovered; it does not by itself make the carrier undetectable.

#### Why This Matters

John Byrne — Irish-American writer and friend of James Joyce — invented the Chaocipher in 1918 and spent four decades trying to convince the US government and AT&T to adopt it. Its design was not publicly available during those proposals. His 1953 autobiography *Silent Years* included challenge ciphertexts; the algorithm became public 57 years later. <svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-scroll"></use></svg>

Historical Context

Byrne promoted Chaocipher for decades after devising it in 1918. His death in 1960 preceded the family’s 2010 donation of the papers to the National Cryptologic Museum. Public disclosure enabled researchers to distinguish the algorithm from its secret initial alphabets and to study specific key-recovery attacks. Earlier secrecy and unaccepted proposals are not evidence of a general security proof.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-gear"></use></svg>
```

How It Works

Two 26-letter alphabets, called the "left" (ciphertext) and "right" (plaintext) wheels, are independently scrambled. To encrypt one letter:

```text
1. Find the plaintext letter on the right wheel.
2. The ciphertext letter is at the same position on the left wheel.
3. Rotate both wheels so the touched letters are at the "zenith" (position 0).
4. Permute the LEFT wheel: take the letter at position 1, slide it into position 13.
5. Permute the RIGHT wheel: rotate it one position, then take the letter at
   position 2 and slide it into position 13.
6. Repeat for the next plaintext letter.
```

The evolving wheels change the substitution after each letter. The widget derives its left alphabet by deduplicating the seed and appending unused A–Z letters; its right alphabet is that sequence rotated by 13. This restricted key setup is a teaching choice, not support for entering two independently chosen historical wheel alphabets.

```text
Widget seed: CHAOCIPHER
```

How It Was Broken

Disclosure (2010)

Algorithm disclosure enabled public analysis

The Byrne family donated the Chaocipher papers to the National Cryptologic Museum in 2010, making the algorithm public after 92 years. Disclosure itself was not a cryptanalytic break; it allowed researchers to study the keyed construction.

Known-Plaintext and In-Depth Attacks

Published key-recovery attacks

A known-plaintext key-recovery method was published in 2010. In 2016, George Lasry, Moshe Rubin, Nils Kopal, and Arno Wacker published a hill-climbing attack against multiple messages encrypted with the same initial settings, plus a known-plaintext attack on short in-depth messages. Their [Cryptologia paper](<https://doi.org/10.1080/01611194.2015.1091797>) establishes weaknesses in the classic cipher; it does not claim a general ciphertext-only break of every isolated message.

#### Why This Matters

The Sheridan Libraries’ account of *Dian bao xin bian* (1909) describes an earlier Danish contribution, refinement in Shanghai in 1872, and Zheng Guanying’s revision in 1881. Chinese telegraph codebooks assign four-digit numbers to the characters included in their tables, allowing messages in Chinese to travel through telegraph networks. These are character encodings, not secret encryption by themselves. Successive editions and regional variants do not necessarily give every character the same number; the sender and receiver must use compatible tables. Chinese Commercial Code numbers also have administrative uses. Hong Kong’s Immigration Department lists a name in Chinese commercial code, where applicable, among identity-card particulars. This is a specific documented use, not evidence that every mainland passport or Taiwanese registration carries the same code field.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-gear"></use></svg>
```

How It Works

The codebook is a giant lookup table. Each included character maps to a four-digit number in the selected table; the operator transmits the digits as Morse code. The receiver looks up each 4-digit group in their copy of the same codebook and recovers the character.

For confidentiality, some systems superenciphered the numeric codegroups. NSA’s account of Viguier’s 1873 proposal describes adding a key number digit by digit modulo 10, without carries. A codebook plus an additive is a broad design pattern also found in other systems; it does not mean all such systems used identical arithmetic, group sizes or key procedures.

The demo uses an invented A–Z table and adds the same numeric input to each four-digit group modulo 10,000, with carries. This is a deliberately small teaching model. It differs from the digitwise historical example and is neither a transcription of a Chinese codebook nor a one-time-pad system.

How It Was (and Was Not) Broken

Public codebook → trivial without superencipherment

Complexity: Lookup only

Civil traffic used the published codebook with no additive. Anyone with a copy could read the messages — the codebook was a transmission convenience, not a cipher.

With additive: depth attack

Condition: shared additive and compatible arithmetic

Reusing an additive exposes relations between codegroups. For this demo, subtracting ciphertext groups modulo 10,000 cancels the shared additive. In a digitwise system, cancellation must instead be performed digit by digit modulo 10. Recovering the actual text still requires codebook information or other constraints; reuse does not automatically solve every message.

##### Why This Matters

On 4 January 2012 a plain image appeared on 4chan reading 'Hello. We are looking for highly intelligent individuals.' Solving it required Caesar-shift decoding, OutGuess image steganography, a book code on the Mabinogion, a call to a Dallas telephone number, a prime-number step, GPS coordinates that pointed to QR-coded posters in cities on several continents, a second book code on William Gibson's *Agrippa (A Book of the Dead)*, and ultimately a Tor hidden service. Two further puzzles ran in 2013 and 2014. The third released digital page images of *Liber Primus* in a runic alphabet. CicadaSolvers’ status account, checked on 26 October 2026, distinguishes a solved 17-page first set from a 58-page second set with two solved pages (56 and 57). “The first 19 pages” is not the correct description. The identity of '3301' has never been confirmed.

Historical Context

The 2012 puzzle alone employed: a Caesar shift, digital steganography (the OutGuess tool), URL discovery, a book code on the Mabinogion — the "Lady of the Fountain" text as printed in Bulfinch's *Mythology* — a telephone recording that set a prime-number task, a second book code on Gibson's *Agrippa (A Book of the Dead)*, and finally an invitation by private email. Solvers who finished within the deadline reported being asked to write essays on cryptography and privacy; some never heard back. Theories about who '3301' was range from a private intelligence-recruitment outfit to a libertarian cryptography collective.

Technical Notes

Cicada combined classical ciphers with steganography, cryptographic signatures, internet services and collaborative puzzle solving. These are distinct tools: a PGP signature can authenticate a message’s key origin without revealing who controls that key. The difficulty of the overall hunt is not a ranking of cryptographic security.

Modern Echo

Cicada illustrates how communities combine several kinds of evidence in a puzzle hunt. Similarities to institutional recruitment challenges do not establish that GCHQ or NSA copied its design. Reports of new solutions or messages require reproducible methods and careful provenance checks.
