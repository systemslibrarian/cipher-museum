The Cyclic World Diffie–Hellman Lives In

### Arithmetic on
a *clock*

A clock counts to twelve and starts over. Modular arithmetic does the same with any modulus. Powers modulo a prime reveal cyclic groups and subgroups; deployed key exchanges often use a large prime-order subgroup rather than every nonzero residue.

**Why this matters.** A primitive root generates every nonzero residue modulo a prime, but finite-field Diffie–Hellman can instead use a generator of a large prime-order subgroup. Group order, parameter selection and validation matter; being a primitive root alone does not establish security.

§1

#### Powers that walk the clock

Fix a prime *p* and a base *g*. Compute g¹, g², g³, … mod p and mark each result on the ring below. If *g* is a primitive root, the marks visit all p−1 positions before returning home — a single unbroken orbit. If not, the orbit closes early, missing most of the clock.

§2

#### The one-way staircase

Computing gˣ mod p is efficient. Recovering an exponent from the result is the discrete logarithm problem. This tiny group is easy to search; suitable large groups make known attacks expensive. The result constrains the exponent modulo the order of g.

x ↦ gˣ mod p easy · gˣ ↦ x hard

Where this touches your keys

##### The original public-key setting

In finite-field Diffie–Hellman, Alice sends gᵃ and Bob sends gᵇ; each computes gᵃᵇ. Computing that value as an eavesdropper is the computational Diffie–Hellman problem. Solving a discrete logarithm would suffice, but discrete-log hardness alone is not a proof of CDH hardness.

Many deployed finite-field DH groups use a safe prime and a large prime-order subgroup. This avoids treating small-order elements as acceptable public keys, provided implementations also apply the protocol's public-key validation rules. Choosing a generator alone does not prevent an active attacker from sending an invalid or small-order value.

See also: [Group Theory](<https://ciphermuseum.com/hall-of-foundations/group-theory.html>) for the general structure, and [Finite Fields](<https://ciphermuseum.com/hall-of-foundations/finite-fields.html>) for the elliptic-curve version.

**Sources and scope:** [RFC 7919 (2016)](<https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7919.html>), standardized finite-field DH groups and validation; Boneh and Shoup, §10.4.2, CDH and discrete logarithms.

The Number Theory Beneath ECC

### The ground beneath
the *curve*

Elliptic-curve cryptography rests on objects far older and stranger than any key exchange. This exhibit walks from a cubic equation to the deepest theorem it touches — and is honest about where the mathematics stops being useful to a cryptographer.

**A note on honesty.** Fermat's Last Theorem — the 100-page proof this exhibit builds toward — has no direct use in cryptography. The link between elliptic curves and modular forms is mathematical context here. ECC’s group law and computational assumptions do not depend on the Modularity Theorem.

§1

#### A curve is a cubic

For this exhibit an elliptic curve is a nonsingular Weierstrass cubic together with a point at infinity. Its equation has rational coefficients, while this smooth plot shows its real solutions. Rational, real and finite-field points are different sets. The name comes from the history of elliptic integrals.

y² = x³ + ax + b

Fix *a* and *b* so the curve is smooth — no cusps, no self-crossings — and you get the graceful looping shape below. What makes it cryptographically interesting is not the shape but a hidden operation living on it: you can *add* two points and land on a third.

§2

#### The group law, drawn by hand

Here is the whole secret. To add points *P* and *Q*: draw the line through them, find the third place it meets the curve, and reflect that point across the x-axis. That reflection is *P + Q*. Tap two points and watch the construction draw itself.

Teaching model using floating-point approximations: point coordinates are computed from the actual group law over the reals and projected onto the display; no cryptographic protocol is implemented.

§3

#### Scalar multiplication and its inverse problem

In an elliptic-curve group, scalar multiplication forms *Q = kP* by adding *k* copies of *P*. Recovering *k* modulo the order of *P* from *P* and *Q* is the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem. Its difficulty depends on the group and parameters.

P, Q known → find k such that Q = kP · difficulty depends on the group

Suitable finite-field elliptic-curve groups support hard discrete-log and related assumptions used by protocols such as ECDH and ECDSA. Not every curve or prime is secure, and visual scattering does not prove hardness. The modularity story below is mathematical context rather than a prerequisite for that security claim.

§4

#### The second face: modular forms

An elliptic curve over ℚ has an L-function built from arithmetic data, including point counts at primes of good reduction. Modular forms are highly symmetric functions on the complex upper half-plane. Their connection is deep number theory, not an extra encryption operation.

The Modularity Theorem associates every elliptic curve over ℚ with a weight-two cuspidal newform whose L-function matches that of the curve. Wiles and Taylor–Wiles established the semistable case in work published in 1995; the general case was completed by 2001. This is an equality of L-functions, not a claim that a zeta function literally is a modular form.

Two traditions that never spoke — point-counting and the analysis of symmetric functions — turned out to be the same language.

§5

#### The rungs to Fermat

Modularity is where Fermat's Last Theorem falls. Not by attacking *aⁿ + bⁿ = cⁿ* directly, but by showing a hypothetical counterexample would build an elliptic curve too strange to exist.

- Frey · 1985the trap A counterexample to Fermat yields a curve y² = x(x + aⁿ)(x − bⁿ) with impossible properties.

- Serre · Ribetthe tightening Made precise: such a curve, if modular, forces a modular form that plainly cannot exist.

- Wiles · 1994the keystone Proved semistable curves are modular — so the Frey curve would be modular — so it cannot exist — so Fermat holds.

The proof runs 109 pages plus a 20-page companion. Its engine is an equality between a deformation ring and a Hecke ring — "R = T" — reached through commutative algebra, not through the curves themselves.

Where this touches your keys

##### Mathematical context, not a security assumption

ECC does not require the Modularity Theorem or Fermat's Last Theorem for its group law, correctness or security assumption. The theorem is valuable mathematical context. Practical ECC depends on finite-field arithmetic, suitable group parameters and protocol-specific computational assumptions; modularity does not prove that those assumptions are hard.

This exhibit is reference and education. For the deployable primitives — ECDH, ECDSA, and post-quantum successors — see [Modern Cryptography](<https://ciphermuseum.com/modern.html>).

#### Primary mathematical sources

Andrew Wiles, [*Modular elliptic curves and Fermat’s Last Theorem*](<https://annals.math.princeton.edu/1995/141-3/p01>), Annals of Mathematics 141 (1995), 443–551; Richard Taylor and Andrew Wiles, [*Ring-theoretic properties of certain Hecke algebras*](<https://annals.math.princeton.edu/1995/141-3/p02>), 553–572. These are the 109-page paper and 20-page companion referenced above. The Conceptual Keystone

### Easy one way,
*hard* the other

Many computational cryptographic constructions depend on functions that are easy to evaluate and difficult to invert on properly sampled inputs. Their security is conjectured, not proved merely by drawing an arrow. Information-theoretic systems such as the one-time pad do not require this computational assumption.

**An assumption, not a proof.** We *believe* these functions are hard to reverse; we cannot prove it. Computational public-key schemes rely on particular hardness assumptions. Some proposed schemes have been broken, so each construction needs its own analysis.

§1

#### Multiply, then try to undo it

Multiplying suitably chosen large primes and attempting to recover them illustrates a candidate one-way relationship. For the tiny values shown, factoring is easy. The plot compares multiplication with a trial-division estimate, not with the fastest known general factoring algorithms.

§2

#### The trapdoor twist

Some public-key constructions add a trapdoor. RSA's relevant operation is modular exponentiation: knowing the prime factors lets the owner compute a private exponent and invert that operation. Decryption is not division of the ciphertext by a prime.

public: n = p·q · trapdoor: the pair (p, q)

Trapdoor functions are one design pattern, not a description of every public-key scheme. Diffie–Hellman key agreement, for example, has a different structure.

Where this touches your keys

##### The bet under everything

Factoring breaks RSA, and solving discrete logs breaks classical DH and ECC. Post-quantum designs use several families, including lattice, code and hash-based assumptions. Security claims must name the particular construction and problem, rather than treating all these as the same trapdoor.

A cryptographic hash is intended to resist preimage search without a secret inversion trapdoor. This does not mean an attacker cannot recover a low-entropy input by guessing. No proof establishes that a concrete hash such as SHA-256 is a one-way function against every efficient attacker.

The Structure Inside ML-KEM & ML-DSA

### Numbers that
are *polynomials*

The lattice exhibits showed why hard problems on a grid protect a key. But the deployed post-quantum standards don't use plain grids — they use lattices built from *polynomials*, where a whole list of numbers multiplies as a single object and wraps around a ring boundary. That structure is what makes ML-KEM fast enough to ship.

**The missing bridge.** Between the finite-fields exhibit (numbers mod q) and the lattice exhibits (geometry) sits this: arithmetic on polynomials whose coefficients are numbers mod q, and which themselves wrap modulo Xⁿ+1. ML-KEM’s security is related to Module-LWE. ML-DSA also relies on a short-integer-solution variant, SelfTargetMSIS; the two schemes do not have identical assumptions.

§1

#### A coefficient list that acts as one number

A polynomial like 3 + 2X + 5X² + X³ is just its list of coefficients, [3, 2, 5, 1]. In the ring R_q = Z_q[X]/(Xⁿ+1) two rules apply: every coefficient is reduced *mod q*, and any power Xⁿ or higher wraps back down — because Xⁿ is defined to equal −1. That single rule, Xⁿ = −1, is what makes the ring "negacyclic."

Xⁿ = −1 → Xⁿ⁺ᵏ wraps to −Xᵏ, coefficients mod q

§2

#### Watch the multiplication wrap

Multiplying two polynomials would normally push degrees up to 2n−2. The ring folds everything above degree n−1 back down with a sign flip, and reduces mod q, so the product is another length-n list. Edit either polynomial's coefficients and watch the product — and the terms that wrapped — recompute.

Prime Density and Key Generation

### How to find a
prime in the *dark*

RSA key generation commonly samples large candidates and tests for primality. The Prime Number Theorem explains their approximate density and therefore the expected number of attempts. It does not itself prove that a particular candidate is prime or impose a fixed running time.

**A key-generation connection.** Prime density explains why random candidate search is practical. Generating a key still requires appropriate primality testing and parameter checks; the other algebraic foundations in this hall are also essential to their respective constructions.

§1

#### Primes thin out — but slowly

Among small numbers, primes are everywhere. As you climb, they thin. The Prime Number Theorem pins down exactly how fast: the count of primes below *x*, written π(x), grows like x⁄ln x. Equivalently, a number near *x* is prime with probability about 1⁄ln x.

π(x) ≈ x / ln x · density near x ≈ 1 / ln x

The slider shows small, exactly countable ranges. Cryptographic primes are vastly larger; the asymptotic theorem helps explain their density, not a direct enumeration on this screen.

§2

#### The keygen coin-flip

For a common two-prime 2048-bit RSA modulus, key generation selects two suitable primes of roughly 1024 bits each and checks the product’s size. The theorem says a random 1024-bit *integer* is prime with probability about 1⁄ln(2¹⁰²⁴) ≈ 1⁄710. Restricting this sampling model to odd candidates approximately doubles the density: roughly **1 in 355 odd candidates** is prime. Sieve out small factors like 3 and 5 first and the rate improves again. So you test a few hundred candidates with a fast primality check, and finish with an expected runtime that depends on the implementation and hardware.

at 1024 bits: ~1 in 710 integers · ~1 in 355 *odd* candidates

Keep the claim narrow: this theorem explains why RSA can *find* its primes quickly. It says nothing about why factoring their product is hard — that bet is made in [The One-Way Function](<https://ciphermuseum.com/hall-of-foundations/one-way-function.html>), and the arithmetic that makes the resulting key *work* is in [Euler & Fermat](<https://ciphermuseum.com/hall-of-foundations/euler-fermat.html>).

Prime density explains why random sampling is practical. Reliable random generation, primality testing, parameter choices and implementation are also necessary; a density theorem is not a complete key-generation algorithm.

Where this touches your keys

The Assumption Beneath Fiat–Shamir

### The oracle that
can't be *predicted*

Much of cryptography leans on a fiction so useful it's treated as bedrock: a hash function that behaves like a perfectly random one. Feed it anything, get back noise you could never have guessed — and the same input always returns the same noise. From that single idea, signatures are built.

**A model, held on purpose.** No real function is a true random oracle. A random-oracle proof studies a complete scheme with access to an ideal random function. Replacing that oracle with a real hash is an additional design step, not an automatic transfer of the proof. The visible avalanche effect below is a much weaker property.

§1

#### Two demands on a hash

Preimage resistance asks that finding an input for a specified digest be infeasible; collision resistance asks that finding any two distinct inputs with one digest be infeasible. Neither property follows merely from outputs looking unrelated to their inputs.

pre-image: given h, find m with H(m)=h · collision: find m₁ ≠ m₂ with H(m₁)=H(m₂)

§2

#### The avalanche

Here's the property you can see. Flip a *single* input bit and, in a good hash, roughly *half* the output bits flip — with no pattern linking which. Toggle any input bit below and watch the change cascade through the mixing rounds. A one-bit nudge should scramble the whole output.

**Not a security test.** A broken hash can still show an avalanche effect. Even a deliberately weak function can pass this visual check while remaining easy to invert or collide. §3

#### From random oracle to signature

The Fiat–Shamir transform replaces the verifier's challenge with a hash of the commitment, public statement and required application context; a signature also binds its message. A proof in the random-oracle model treats that hash as an ideal random function. Correct transcript binding and the proof's assumptions matter, and substituting a real hash does not automatically transfer the proof.

A Fiat–Shamir signature binds the message and required transcript context into a hash-derived challenge. Unforgeability belongs to the complete construction under its stated assumptions; it is not a property of the challenge alone.

§4

#### Why abort a signature attempt?

ML-DSA forms a response shaped like *z = y + c·s*: a random mask plus a secret-dependent shift. Unlike uniform masking around a finite group, a mask drawn from a bounded interval has edges. Publishing a response near an edge can reveal information about that shift.

**A scalar example.** Choose the integer mask *y* uniformly from −4 through 4, and let the shift *t* be −1, 0 or 1. The response is *z = y + t*. These are inclusive integer ranges:

- For *t = −1*, responses range from −5 through 3.

- For *t = 0*, responses range from −4 through 4.

- For *t = 1*, responses range from −3 through 5.

Seeing *z = 5* identifies *t = 1*. Instead, discard the attempt unless *−3 ≤ z ≤ 3*, then resample the mask. For every allowed shift, exactly seven of the nine masks survive. Conditional on acceptance, each of the seven responses has probability 1/7, independent of the shift. The common interior removes this edge information.

This illustrates one reason for Fiat–Shamir *with aborts*. Actual ML-DSA operates on polynomial vectors, imposes a strict response-norm bound and performs further rejection checks. The scalar example is not its signing algorithm or a security proof. Compare the [Schnorr identification example](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/zero-knowledge-proofs.html>), where masking uses modular arithmetic.

Explore the existing [Dilithium Reject lab](<https://systemslibrarian.github.io/crypto-lab-dilithium-reject/>): “Why Each Check Exists” explains the bounds, and “Watch the Loop” shows signing attempts. Retry histograms alone do not demonstrate exploitable secret leakage.

Where this touches your keys

##### Used by several signature families

Schnorr-style signatures and ML-DSA use Fiat–Shamir ideas. ML-DSA uses SHAKE-based hashing and a construction often described as Fiat–Shamir with aborts. SLH-DSA belongs to a hash-based signature family with different assumptions and tradeoffs. These are distinct constructions, not one security argument applied to every signature.

What "Provably Secure" Actually Claims

### Standing on a
*borrowed* difficulty

Cryptographers rarely prove a scheme is hard to break. They prove a conditional implication: that breaking it would let you solve a problem everyone already believes is hard. Security is borrowed from a difficulty we trust — and the loan is made by a *reduction*.

**A conditional claim.** A proof names an assumption, adversary model and quantitative reduction loss. It neither proves that assumption nor licenses reversing the reduction arrow.

§1

#### The contrapositive machine

Suppose a precisely defined reduction could turn a successful scheme-breaker into a solver for a specified hard problem. If the transformation preserved sufficient running time and success probability, the problem's assumed hardness would bound the attacker. The animation is a hypothetical logic diagram, not an implementation or a proof that RSA inversion is equivalent to factoring.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="curve" viewBox="0 0 700 420" id="red" role="img" aria-label="A reduction: a scheme-breaker is used to build a factoring machine."></svg>
```

Run the reduction Reset

Suppose a break exists…

Read it as a machine: feed a hard factoring instance in, wrap it as a fake scheme challenge, let the hypothetical breaker crack it, and read the factors back out. If the reduction is efficient enough, a sufficiently fast breaker would contradict the specified factoring-hardness assumption.

§2

#### The chain of trust

The direction of the implication matters. A reduction using an attacker to solve a problem supports a conditional security claim. The opposite direction—using a problem solver to break a scheme—shows a possible attack but is not that security proof. A broken assumption can invalidate a proof without automatically yielding an attack on every scheme.

- RSAknown attack directionfactoring the modulus breaks RSA. General RSA inversion is not known to be equivalent to factoring.

- ECC · DHscheme-specific assumptionsCDH, DDH or other group assumptions, according to the protocol and security goal. A discrete-log solver is enough to break DH.

- ML-KEM · ML-DSAmodule-lattice assumptionsspecific Module-LWE and Module-SIS-related problems and additional construction assumptions; not an equivalence to arbitrary exact SVP.

A reduction says how an attacker would imply a solver under stated conditions. It does not require an attacker to literally run that solver first.

Where this touches your keys

##### Why anyone trusts a primitive

Public-key security estimates combine formal definitions, conditional reductions and concrete cryptanalysis. A reduction has an attack model, parameter restrictions and a loss in time or success probability; it may not be tight. No reduction proves the underlying hardness assumption itself.

AES is supported by decades of public cryptanalysis rather than a proof from an independently established hard problem. Higher-level symmetric constructions can still have reductions to assumptions about their block cipher or hash. Both the mathematical model and practical attack evidence matter.

Modular Arithmetic

The Caesar cipher wraps around the alphabet using modulo 26. `(position + shift) mod 26`. Modular arithmetic appears throughout cryptography; AES uses binary finite-field operations rather than addition modulo 26.

Coordinate Encoding

Polybius’s coordinate notation separates a symbol into row and column information. Later fractionating ciphers rearrange such coordinates; modern bitslicing is a different implementation technique.

 Union Cryptography

- **Stager Cipher:** Purpose-built for telegraph security. Route transposition + code-word substitution evolved continuously throughout the war, through a series of numbered cipher books ending with Cipher No. 4 (1865).

- **Professional management:** The U.S. Military Telegraph Corps, under Stager and Eckert, controlled all cipher usage. Operators were trained and supervised.

- **Reported operational success:** Historical accounts credit the Union system’s protection of traffic. This does not prove mathematical immunity or establish the fate of every intercepted telegram.

 Confederate Cryptography

- **Repeating-key substitution:** Confederate use preceded Kasiski’s 1863 publication. Documented Union solutions do not establish that the analysts used his method.

- **Key reuse:** A recovered key could expose other traffic using that key. It did not automatically reveal every Confederate military message.

- **Dictionary codes:** Correspondents needed matching editions and lookup conventions. Kahn documents specific dictionary arrangements; an unsupported story about two generals using mismatched editions is not evidence.

🏛️

**Reading the evidence:** Word-route ciphers, codeword substitutions and dictionary references are different procedures.  Al-Kindi

~850 AD

**Person:** Abu Yusuf Yaqub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi, Baghdad

**Technique:** Frequency analysis

Al-Kindi’s surviving treatise gives an early systematic account of frequency analysis. A fixed letter substitution preserves counts, allowing language statistics and word patterns to guide a search. That does not mean every ciphertext has enough information for a unique solution.

💡

Letter frequencies vary by language, genre and sample length. Comparing them can suggest candidate substitutions; words and higher-order patterns help test those guesses. The most common symbol need not encode the most common letter in a short message.

 Thomas Phelippes and Mary Queen of Scots[Full exhibit →](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/babington.html>)

1586

**Person:** Thomas Phelippes, agent of Sir Francis Walsingham

**Technique:** Frequency analysis + contextual knowledge

Mary's encrypted correspondence with Babington Plot conspirators was intercepted and decoded. She was executed February 8, 1587. The break is one of history's clearest examples of cryptanalysis determining a human life.

 Étienne Bazeries and the Great Cipher

1893

**Person:** Commandant Étienne Bazeries (1846–1931), French Army cryptanalyst

**Technique:** Syllabic-substitution analysis on a 587-symbol nomenclator, with contextual cribbing against historical Louis XIV correspondence

In the 1890s Bazeries reconstructed a Rossignol-era nomenclator using syllable hypotheses, contextual readings and repeated groups. The royal systems developed during the seventeenth century; Bonaventure should not be credited with creating one alongside his father in the 1620s. Kahn describes homophones, nulls and an erasure instruction affecting the previous sign. These special entries complicate analysis, but no one trap explains the whole history of the decipherment.

Bazeries’s decipherment of Louis XIV correspondence also led him to propose an identity for the Man in the Iron Mask. That historical interpretation is disputed; deciphering a letter does not settle every inference about its subject. He also designed a cipher cylinder and published *Les Chiffres Secrets Dévoilés* (1901 edition).

💡

The Great Cipher break is the canonical example of **cryptanalysis as historiography**: the tool was invented to decrypt diplomacy in real time, but its lasting payoff was unlocking a 200-year-old archive. See the exhibit: [Hall II · Great Cipher (17th century)](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/great-cipher.html>).

 Room 40 and the Zimmermann Telegram

1917

**People:** Room 40, British Naval Intelligence

**Technique:** Code book reconstruction from partial recoveries

Germany secretly offered Mexico an alliance against the United States. Britain decoded it, waited strategically, then shared it with Washington. The telegram helped bring America into WWI. The war ended 20 months later.

 The Polish Breaking of Enigma

1932

**People:** Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki, Henryk Zygalski

**Technique:** Permutation group mathematics + known message indicators

Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski combined mathematical analysis, intercepted indicators and intelligence supplied through French cooperation to reconstruct and attack military Enigma. They shared their results with French and British colleagues near Warsaw in July 1939. See [GCHQ’s account of that meeting](<https://www.gchq.gov.uk/information/the-pyry-forest-meeting>).

🇵🇱

Rejewski's breakthrough came from analyzing the message indicator system — the first six characters of each Enigma message. By collecting enough indicators, he found patterns that revealed the mathematical structure of the rotors. Pure mathematics, applied to an engineering problem, by a 27-year-old mathematician.

 Turing, Welchman, and the Bombe

1940

**People:** Alan Turing, Gordon Welchman, engineering teams and bombe operators

**Technique:** Crib-based electromechanical search

British bombe machines tested constraints derived from possible plaintext, building on the Polish breakthrough and later Allied work. ULTRA intelligence contributed to wartime operations. Estimates of how much it shortened the war are counterfactual judgments, not measured results.

 Tiltman, Tutte, Flowers, and Colossus[Full exhibit →](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/bill-tutte.html>)

1941–1944

**People:** John Tiltman (message/key recovery), Bill Tutte and the Research Section (structure), Tommy Flowers and team (engineering)

**Technique:** Statistical wheel-pattern analysis

Tiltman recovered messages and key from a repeated-key transmission in 1941. Tutte and the Research Section inferred the machine’s structure by early 1942. Flowers and his team later built Colossus, operational in February 1944, to accelerate statistical wheel-setting.

 William Friedman and Purple

1940

**Person:** William F. Friedman, US Army Signal Intelligence

**Technique:** Statistical analysis + machine reconstruction

The SIS team reconstructed PURPLE’s logical behavior and achieved its major breakthrough in September 1940. It produced valuable Japanese diplomatic intelligence before Pearl Harbor, but those diplomatic messages did not disclose the operational attack plan. Diplomatic and naval intelligence must not be treated as interchangeable.

⚠️

Breaking an encryption system does not guarantee access to every relevant message, a correct translation or the right interpretation. Intelligence collection, distribution and operational decision-making are separate stages.

 The Navajo Code Talkers

1942–1945

**People:** 420 Navajo Marines, US Marine Corps

**Cipher:** Navajo language plus a constructed military code

Navajo Marines used a specially developed military vocabulary and alphabet-spelling code as well as their language. The original 29 code developers received Congressional Gold Medals in 2001; later code talkers received silver medals. The code’s reported wartime success is a historical result, not a mathematical proof of secrecy.

 Joan Clarke and Naval Enigma[Full exhibit →](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/joan-clarke.html>)

1940–1945

**Person:** Joan Elisabeth Lowther Clarke (later Murray), 1917–1996

**Technique:** Banburismus — Bayesian sequential analysis of Enigma indicators

Recruited from Cambridge mathematics in 1940, Clarke became one of the most productive cryptanalysts in Hut 8 and rose to deputy head — extraordinary in a service that paid female staff at clerical rates and barred them from "officer" titles. She was a fluent practitioner of Banburismus, the Bayesian sequential procedure invented by Turing to slash the Bombe's workload by ranking which wheel orders to test first. On the most stubborn naval traffic she shared the load with Turing himself; on shorter runs she worked alone.

Clarke’s Naval Enigma work contributed to intelligence used in the Atlantic campaign. Access varied by network and period, including the 1942 U-boat blackout. She received the MBE in 1946. Her postwar career included a move to Scotland after marriage in 1952, a return to Cheltenham in 1962, formal retirement in 1977 and later clerical work. The linked biography distinguishes those dates and supplies the sources.

 Bill Tutte: Inferring the Lorenz Structure[Full exhibit →](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/bill-tutte.html>)

1941–1942

**Person:** William Thomas "Bill" Tutte, 1917–2002, Cambridge graph theorist

**Technique:** Statistical period-finding on a 4,000-character depth produced by an operator's August 1941 mistake

After a repeated-key Lorenz transmission in August 1941, John Tiltman recovered plaintext and key material. Tutte and the Research Section analysed the resulting stream and inferred the twelve-wheel structure by early 1942. This work distinguished five chi wheels, five psi wheels and two motor wheels, enabling later statistical attacks without first possessing the machine.

Tutte’s statistical work helped make machine-assisted Tunny attacks possible. Colossus was a collective engineering and operational achievement, and manual work continued alongside it. Tutte later made major contributions to graph theory and combinatorics. His codebreaking role remained little known for many years; the Tutte Institute for Mathematics and Computing in Canada commemorates his work.

💡

See also: [Hall VII · Lorenz exhibit](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/lorenz.html>) for the machine; Story 6 above for Tommy Flowers and Colossus.

 Elizebeth Smith Friedman: Rum-Runners and Nazi Spies[Full exhibit →](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/elizebeth-friedman.html>)

1923–1946

**Person:** Elizebeth Smith Friedman, 1892–1980

**Technique:** Hand cryptanalysis of commercial codes, polyalphabetics, and one-part nomenclators across 12,000+ messages

Elizebeth Friedman developed cryptanalytic methods at Riverbank and later led important Coast Guard work against smuggling traffic. Her team’s work included expert testimony and wartime analysis of clandestine communications. American cryptanalysis had earlier practitioners too; it was not invented by one couple.

During WWII Friedman’s Coast Guard team analyzed clandestine networks in the Western Hemisphere. Its cryptanalytic contributions are distinct from the investigative work that disrupted those networks. Her archival papers and the biographical sources linked in the exhibit help recover that history.

 Agnes Meyer Driscoll: Madame X of US Naval Intelligence[Full exhibit →](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/agnes-driscoll.html>)

1920s–1940s

**Person:** Agnes Meyer Driscoll, 1889–1971, US Navy civilian cryptanalyst

**Technique:** Analysis of Japanese naval codes and additive encipherment; manual codebook reconstruction

Driscoll joined the Navy in 1918 and became an important analyst of Japanese naval systems, including the Red Book, Blue Book and early JN-25. She helped train later Navy cryptanalysts. Her JN-25 work preceded the Pacific teams’ 1942 Midway intelligence successes; wartime assignments also included work against German Enigma.

Driscoll worked on the Navy’s CM cipher machine before leaving briefly for the Hebern company in 1923. Her career combined machine knowledge and manual analysis. See the [NSA biographical account](<https://www.nsa.gov/Press-Room/Digital-Media-Center/Biographies/Biography-View-Page/Article/1623020/agnes-meyer-driscoll/>).

 Leo Marks and the SOE Agent Codes[Full exhibit →](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/leo-marks.html>)

1942–1945

**Person:** Leopold Samuel "Leo" Marks, 1920–2001, Head of Codes, Special Operations Executive

**Technique:** Prepared keying material, letter one-time pads and communications security checks

Marks worked on SOE agents’ communications, including efforts to recover messages corrupted by operator errors and reduce the need for dangerous retransmission. Prepared keys and agreed security checks addressed different operational risks; no indicator scheme guaranteed survival under error, interception or coercion.

Marks advocated prepared worked-out keys and letter one-time pads as improvements to SOE communications. These are distinct methods, and silk describes a carrier rather than a cryptographic construction. His memoir, *Between Silk and Cyanide: A Codemaker’s War, 1941–1945* (1998 edition), gives his retrospective account. A poem-derived transposition key is not a random one-time pad.

💡

See also: [Hall IX · One-Time Pad](<https://ciphermuseum.com/ciphers/one-time-pad.html>) for the mathematics of letter one-time pads; silk could also carry other kinds of prepared keying material.

 VENONA and the Soviet OTP

1943–1980

**People:** Meredith Gardner and the US Army Signal Intelligence Service

**Technique:** Depth attack on reused pad pages

VENONA analysts exploited duplicated Soviet pad material. These messages used decimal code groups and no-carry arithmetic, so subtracting aligned ciphertext digits cancels the reused pad; it is not literally bytewise XOR. Recovering code groups and identifying people required further analysis. See the [NSA’s VENONA history](<https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/about/cryptologic-heritage/historical-figures-publications/publications/coldwar/venona_story.pdf>).

⚡

The pad-production and distribution process violated the one-use condition. Negative results against a correctly used one-time pad say nothing about traffic encrypted with duplicated pages. VENONA’s partial recoveries came from this operational weakness, not a contradiction of Shannon’s theorem.

 Oranchak, Blake, Van Eycke and the Zodiac Z-340

2020

**People:** David Oranchak, Sam Blake, Jarl Van Eycke

 Frank Rowlett and the SIS Purple Team

1939–1940

**Person:** Frank B. Rowlett, US Army Signal Intelligence Service

**Technique:** Stepping-switch analysis using traffic and related plaintext evidence

Frank Rowlett led day-to-day SIS work against PURPLE with colleagues including Genevieve Grotjan and William Friedman. Statistical relationships in traffic and related plaintext evidence helped reconstruct the machine’s behavior without a captured PURPLE machine.

Rowlett also contributed to the development of SIGABA. No successful enemy wartime cryptanalytic break of that system is publicly documented; that is narrower than a guarantee that the design can never be attacked.

 Genevieve Grotjan and September 20, 1940

1940

**Person:** Genevieve Grotjan Feinstein, SIS junior cryptanalyst

**Technique:** Pattern recognition across aligned worksheets

On the afternoon of September 20, 1940, Grotjan asked Rowlett to look at a worksheet she had prepared. Aligning intercept plaintext-ciphertext pairs, she had identified a recurring relationship in the consonant cipher alphabet — the alignment that revealed how Purple's stepping switches were wired. Rowlett later described the moment as “one of the great breaks of the war.” Grotjan had been with SIS for less than two years.

💡

Grotjan also contributed to the work that became VENONA. She left government service in 1947, before NSA was established, and later taught mathematics. See the [NSA biography](<https://www.nsa.gov/Press-Room/Digital-Media-Center/Biographies/Biography-View-Page/Article/1621585/genevieve-grotjan-feinstein/>).

 Boris Hagelin and the M-209

1934–1983

**Person:** Boris C. W. Hagelin, Swedish-born cipher-machine designer

**Contribution:** Pin-and-lug rotor mechanics for hand-portable cipher machines

Boris Hagelin developed commercially important pin-and-lug machines, including the family from which the M-209 was derived.  The Copiale Team

2011

**People:** Kevin Knight (USC ISI), Beáta Megyesi and Christiane Schaefer (Uppsala University)

**Technique:** Transcription, statistics and manual cryptanalysis

Knight, Megyesi and Schaefer combined transcription, statistical exploration and manual cryptanalysis to decipher the Copiale manuscript in 2011. Early automatic hypotheses did not directly solve it: recognizing how ordinary Roman letters and abstract symbols functioned was crucial. See their [original paper](<https://aclanthology.org/W11-1202/>).

 The Chaocipher Verifiers

2010–2014

**People:** Moshe Rubin (algorithm reconstruction, 2010), George Lasry (cryptanalytic verification)

**Technique:** Documentary reconstruction + simulated annealing

Byrne kept Chaocipher’s mechanism secret. The family’s 2010 donation made its algorithm available; later published key-recovery work studied attacks under specified message and crib conditions. ### Mechanical Cipher Machines

When cryptography became hardware — and hardware changed history

From Alberti’s fifteenth-century disk to twentieth-century rotor and pin-and-lug devices, this hall explores the mechanisation of cipher operations. Interception, cryptanalysis, engineering and interpretation were all needed to turn enemy communications into intelligence; machines alone did not win wars or decrypt every message.

**The mechanical advantage:** Hand ciphers require operators to look up tables, count positions, and write carefully. ### Modern Cryptography

Different cryptographic tasks, constructions and security assumptions

The earlier halls distinguish cryptanalytic breaks, operational misuse, obsolescence, and unresolved systems; a correctly used one-time pad remains unbreakable. The constructions here serve different purposes. DES and AES are symmetric block ciphers; DES is now unsafe because its 56-bit key is too small. RSA supports distinct public-key encryption and signature schemes, with appropriate encoding and padding such as OAEP and PSS. Diffie–Hellman is key agreement, and SHA-256 is a hash function. These building blocks explain encryption, shared-secret establishment, signatures, and integrity; none alone provides a complete secure system.

For the broader landscape — ChaCha20, ECDH, post-quantum standardisation, and the cipher-vs-protocol taxonomy — see the [Modern Cryptography wing](<https://ciphermuseum.com/modern.html>). ### The Polyalphabetic Revolution

"Le chiffre indéchiffrable" — The unbreakable cipher. Until it wasn't.

Polyalphabetic systems use changing substitution alphabets. They obscure simple letter-frequency relationships, but repeating keys create structure that analysts can exploit. Bellaso, Vigenère, Babbage, Kasiski and Friedman contributed different constructions or methods; the history is more varied than a single cipher remaining universally trusted for 300 years.

The Kasiski Examination

The same plaintext sequence under the same repeating-key alignment produces the same ciphertext. Repeated ciphertext sequences can therefore suggest distances divisible by the period. Index of Coincidence

The index of coincidence measures the chance that two positions chosen without replacement contain the same letter. English samples often lie near 0.066, while independent uniform A–Z samples approach 1/26. Repeating-key substitution can move the overall score toward the latter, but length, language and key period matter: IC supplies evidence, not a certain cipher identification.

Vigenère → Stream Ciphers

Vigenère repeats a short sequence of letter shifts. ChaCha20 instead generates pseudorandom bit blocks from a key, nonce and counter, then combines them with plaintext using XOR. Its security is computational, and its usage rules prevent keystream reuse. “Non-repeating” does not mean that individual output blocks can never coincide.

Running Key → One-Time Pad

The running key cipher is one step from the one-time pad. The difference: a book passage is not random. If the source text has linguistic structure, that structure leaks into the ciphertext and enables statistical attacks.

Keys, Periods and Usage Limits

The Vigenère lesson: short repeating keys are fatal. Modern systems can reuse a suitably strong key for multiple messages, but must respect each scheme’s nonce, counter, and usage limits. Key derivation can separate session keys; it does not automatically guarantee unique key material.

Frequency Analysis

Al-Kindi's 850 AD technique. Monoalphabetic substitution preserves language-frequency patterns. With enough suitable ciphertext, frequency counts and word patterns can reveal the substitution; short messages need not have a unique solution. The Caesar cipher is a trivial special case.

Digraph Encryption → AES Blocks

Diffusion

Diffusion spreads the influence of input information across output components. A pure transposition only moves whole symbols and preserves their frequencies. AES combines permutation and linear mixing with nonlinear substitution and key addition.

 Hall IX · 1882 – Present

### The Unbreakable

Where classical cryptography reached its limits — and mathematics drew the line

Many historical ciphers succumbed to cryptanalysis; others were exposed by operational mistakes, became obsolete, or remain unresolved. Claude Shannon proved perfect secrecy for a correctly used one-time pad: a secret, uniformly random, independent message-length key used once. Its demanding key-distribution requirements limit its use. This hall examines that guarantee and how pad reuse undermined it.

What came next — AES, RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and the systems that protect the digital world — lives in the [Modern Cryptography](<https://ciphermuseum.com/modern.html>) wing.

The Mathematics of Secrecy

#### Claude Shannon and Perfect Secrecy

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-chart"></use></svg>
```

Shannon's 1949 Proof

Perfect secrecy means observing ciphertext does not change the prior probability of any plaintext: P(M=m | C=c) = P(M=m), for ciphertexts with nonzero probability. Plaintexts need not be equally likely beforehand. An independently uniform one-time pad meets this definition; it is not the only possible perfectly secret construction. Encryption alone does not authenticate a message.

For example, if a message was more likely before interception, it remains more likely afterwards. The ciphertext does not improve that prior guess. This guarantee concerns message content within the defined message space; length and other traffic metadata may still be visible.

Source: Claude E. Shannon, *Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems* (1949), §10, pp. 679–682. Shannon also describes perfectly secret systems using Latin squares.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-diamond"></use></svg>
```

Why Its Use Is Limited

A one-time pad has demanding operational requirements:

- The key must be as long as the message — a 1GB file needs a 1GB key

- The key must be truly random — not pseudorandom

- Key distribution must be perfectly secure — if the key channel isn't secure, neither is the cipher

- Every key used once means you can never reuse, recycle, or re-derive it

⚡

**VENONA:** US Army analysts, and later successor organizations including NSA, exploited reused Soviet additive key material. The historical traffic used decimal subtraction without borrowing, not binary XOR; the exhibit shows both the principle and a decimal teaching exercise.

Why Classical Ciphers Failed

#### Shannon's Two Principles — and How Modern Crypto Achieves Them

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-puzzle"></use></svg>
```

 Confusion

Confusion complicates the relationship between the key and ciphertext statistics. For a Caesar shift, C = P + k mod 26, one aligned plaintext–ciphertext letter pair reveals k. Shannon’s term is a design principle, not a universal numerical score.

**Modern solution:** AES uses a fixed, public nonlinear S-box together with key addition and diffusion over multiple rounds. The S-box alone is a known byte substitution; its output determines its input. Security belongs to the complete construction, not secrecy of that table.

[Inline SVG: original diagram/icon markup]

```html
<svg class="icon" width="17" height="17"><use href="../images/icons.svg#i-globe"></use></svg>
```

 Diffusion

Spreading the influence of each plaintext bit across many ciphertext bits. *Caesar does not spread changes between letters* — changing one letter changes exactly one ciphertext letter. Frequency analysis exploits this directly.

**Modern comparison:** AES combines nonlinear substitution, key mixing and linear diffusion. A full-cipher avalanche measurement describes behavior across inputs, not a promise that every change flips exactly half the bits.

The Complete Journey

#### From Caesar to AES — What Each Generation Taught the Next

|  Classical Cipher Type |  Fatal Weakness |  Modern Solution |  Modern Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Caesar / Substitution | Frequency analysis — letter mapping preserved | Repeated substitution, diffusion and key mixing | AES SubBytes |
| Monoalphabetic | 26! keys but relabel the same frequency counts | Fixed public S-box combined with round-key addition | AES with round keys |
| Polyalphabetic / Vigenère | Repeating key creates detectable periodicity | Pseudorandom keystreams with unique nonce/counter inputs under a key; output bytes can repeat | ChaCha20, AES-CTR |
| Transposition | Letters preserved, just rearranged — anagram attacks work | Substitution and linear diffusion across rounds | AES ShiftRows + MixColumns |
| Playfair / Hill (block) | Small blocks leak digraph statistics; linear algebra solvable | 128-bit blocks, non-linear operations, key mixing | AES (128-bit block) |
| Military layered (ADFGVX) | Substitution + transposition — each layer still attackable | AES uses 10, 12 or 14 rounds; the final round omits MixColumns. Inversion with the key is efficient | AES |
| One-Time Pad | Impractical key management — reuse is catastrophic | Computational security with short keys under stated assumptions; not perfect secrecy | AES modes; authenticated X25519-based protocols |

🎓

**The museum’s lesson:** State the security goal and assumptions explicitly. Perfect secrecy, computational security, authenticity and implementation resistance are different claims. Real systems need appropriate parameters, secure key handling and careful implementation; they do not invariably fail loudly when misused.
