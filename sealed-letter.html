The four slips

Follow the marks

Slip 1 · After chapter 1The small key

The first slip has travelled separately from the letter. Its numbers disguise letters, except for two groups that stand for whole words. Someone has helpfully pencilled in an example: 31 24 53 means CAR.

31 08 42 17 24 53 19

08 = O; 17 = P; 19 = E; 24 = A; 31 = C; 42 = M; 53 = R; 65 = TO; 90 = THE.

Use the key to recover the first word. Keep every answer in order; together, the four slips make one instruction.

A nudge

Treat each two-digit group as one entry in the key. The first two groups give CO.

Reveal this slip’s answer

COMPARE. 31, 08, 42, 17, 24, 53 and 19 give C, O, M, P, A, R and E. The unused whole-word groups show how a small code vocabulary can sit beside an alphabet.

A miniature invented nomenclator, prompted by chapter 1. It is not Mary Stuart’s historical cipher.

Slip 2 · After chapter 3The clerk’s route

The second slip contains no disguised letters. The clerk has changed their order. A note on the envelope gives the route: start at the top left, read DOWN to the bottom, then start at the top of the next column to the right.

Read down each column, left to right
TCI
HOE
EPS

Read all three columns in that order. Restore the spaces to recover two words.

A nudge

The first column reads T, H, E. Move to the top of the middle column next.

Reveal this slip’s answer

THE COPIES. The columns give THE, COP and IES. Joined and respaced, they read THE COPIES. No letter has changed; its position has.

An invented route puzzle, prompted by chapter 3. It is not a reconstruction of a Union Army message.

Slip 3 · After chapter 14The reused strip

Two slips used the same six secret numbers. Here, letters become numbers (A = 01 through Z = 26). The sender adds one secret number to each letter number. If a sum reaches 100, subtract 100. All the sums in this example stay below 100.

Known letters: L E T T E R

Letter numbers: 12 05 20 20 05 18

First message: 53 22 82 28 40 38

Second message: 43 22 68 23 53 25

The first message is known to say LETTER. Subtract its letter numbers from its sent numbers to recover the secret strip. Subtract that strip from the second message, then turn the results back into letters. For the first position: 53 minus 12 gives the secret number 41; 43 minus 41 gives 02, or B.

A nudge

The recovered strip is 41 17 62 08 35 20. Use it once more on the second message.

Reveal this slip’s answer

BEFORE. Removing the recovered strip gives 02 05 06 15 18 05: BEFORE. Knowing one message helps expose the other because the secret numbers were reused.

A tiny arithmetic illustration prompted by chapter 14, not a VENONA intercept or a secure encryption tool. Actual one-time-pad protection depends on a random secret pad used only once.

Slip 4 · After chapter 17The letters that do nothing

The last slip appears cluttered. Its note is blunt: the lower-case letters are padding. They carry nothing. Keep only the CAPITAL letters, in their existing order. The spaces on the slip were added to mislead.

aYbO cUdT eRfU gShT iTjH kElM

Remove the padding and the false spaces. Restore ordinary word breaks to recover the final three words.

A nudge

The first three capitals are Y, O and U. Keep working from left to right.

Reveal this slip’s answer

YOU TRUST THEM. The capitals spell YOUTRUSTTHEM. The spacing becomes YOU TRUST THEM. The familiar-looking lower-case letters were a distraction.

An invented null-letter puzzle prompted by the change of assumptions in chapter 17. It does not reproduce the Copiale cipher’s symbol system.

The envelope is open · A fictional evidence case

The room that changed

A message orders the transfer of a sealed case inside a museum. Two copies of the instruction disagree. Someone has accused Ellis, the keeper of the keys, of arranging a theft. You have four documents. What do they actually establish?

All people, records and events in this exercise are invented. The times below are claims in the records, not independently verified facts.

A · Dispatch carbon · Marked 08:10

The retained copy

MOVE THE SEALED CASE TO ROOM FOUR AT NINE. KEEP BOTH KEYS WITH ELLIS.

Filed in the dispatch tray. No signature.

B · Delivery copy · Marked 08:25

The delivered copy

MOVE THE SEALED CASE TO ROOM FIVE AT NINE. KEEP BOTH KEYS WITH ELLIS.

Found beside the receiving desk. No signature.

C · Handling log

An opened envelope

08:17 — Envelope received, outer seal intact.
08:20 — Envelope opened for routine inspection. Initial: R.
08:25 — Copy placed at receiving desk.

The log does not identify who wrote either copy.

D · Inventory note · Marked 09:12

The case is still here

Sealed case present in room four. Seal appears intact. Contents not checked.

There is no accompanying record for room five.

1. Compare the wording of A and B. What changed?

2. Choose the conclusion these records can support.

Investigation complete

A difference established. An accusation unproved.

You have established the change from FOUR to FIVE. The handling log suggests a point to investigate, but opening an envelope does not establish that R changed its contents. Having keys does not establish that Ellis stole anything. Even the inventory note has a limit: it records an apparently intact seal, not a check of the contents.

The next useful evidence: the dispatch original, the author of each copy, a documented chain of custody and a check of the case’s contents. A copying error, a changed instruction and a deliberate alteration remain different explanations. Nothing here lets you choose confidently among them.

This is the decision at the heart of the book: what has been read, what has been proved, and what still needs another source?
