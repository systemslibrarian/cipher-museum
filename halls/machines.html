Hall VII · 1467 – 1950s

Mechanical Cipher Machines

When cryptography became hardware — and hardware changed history

From Alberti’s fifteenth-century disk to twentieth-century rotor and pin-and-lug devices, this hall explores the mechanisation of cipher operations. Interception, cryptanalysis, engineering and interpretation were all needed to turn enemy communications into intelligence; machines alone did not win wars or decrypt every message.

146717951932–19451947–1950s
⚙️

The mechanical advantage: Hand ciphers require operators to look up tables, count positions, and write carefully. Mechanical devices automate the cipher operation — dramatically increasing speed and reducing errors. The tradeoff: a captured machine reveals everything about the cipher structure. Security must then rely entirely on the key settings.

The Arc of History

From Bronze Disk to Electronic Computer

Alberti → Vigenère → Enigma

Alberti’s disk, repeating-key tables and electrical rotor machines all illustrate changing substitutions. Their mechanisms and historical development differ: the familiar repeating-key table is associated with Bellaso (1553), while Vigenère described an autokey system (1586).

Lorenz → Colossus → Computing

Tiltman’s manual recovery and Tutte’s structural analysis came first. Later statistical searches over Lorenz wheel settings benefited from electronic counting: Colossus was operational in February 1944. This was an important early application of programmable electronics.

Physical Keys → Digital Keys

Enigma required physical rotor choices, positions, ring settings and plugboard connections. Modern systems use several kinds of keys and lengths, with different distribution and authentication methods. Both need disciplined management of secret material.
