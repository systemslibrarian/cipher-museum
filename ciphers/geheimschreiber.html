The people behind daily codebreaking

A structural breakthrough still left daily work. Fredrik Wallin’s account, based on FRA archives, describes this operating chain:

  1. Intercept: collect the encrypted traffic.
  2. Recover the daily key: determine the settings needed to read it.
  3. Operate App: enter ciphertext into the decryption apparatus and monitor synchronization. App meant “apparatus,” not a software application.
  4. Prepare the report: assemble the plaintext, clean up its language and type the final report.

Women performed essential operating and interception work; some later led interception shifts. On 1 July 1942, women were 193 of FRA’s 287 civilian staff—about 67%. Another 113 seconded military personnel brought the agency total to 400. The 67% figure refers to civilians, not the whole agency.

Source: Fredrik Wallin, “We just did it!” (HistoCrypt 2023), pp. 1–4. These staffing totals concern FRA as a whole, not just its T52 team. This panel summarizes Wallin’s archival research; it does not claim an independent archive review.

Why This Matters

While Lorenz SZ40/42 (“Tunny”) carried Hitler’s strategic traffic, the Siemens & Halske T52 — codenamed “Sturgeon” at Bletchley Park — carried Luftwaffe and Wehrmacht command traffic over leased Swedish telephone lines from 1940. Swedish access to those circuits supplied material for cryptanalysis.

In 1940 Beurling worked with the Swedish Defence Staff’s cryptographic service, the predecessor of FRA as an independent agency. His approximately two-week breakthrough, assisted by Hans Rudberg and Bertil Nyman, was followed by continuing key-recovery and decryption work. The FRA account distinguishes these stages.

How It Works

The T52 family used teleprinter ciphers; later T52d/e models added irregular stepping. Plaintext is encoded in 5-bit Baudot characters and combined with a keystream generated by ten wheels with pairwise-coprime lengths (47, 53, 59, 61, 64, 65, 67, 69, 71, 73). The following two-bank description belongs to this teaching model, not a wiring specification for every T52 variant:

  • Five wheels generate an additive keystream that shifts each character.
  • Five wheels select, per character, one of several keyed substitution permutations applied after the additive shift.

In the original T52 the substitution is a bit-permutation on the 5-bit Baudot code. The educational demo above operates on the 26-letter alphabet using mod-26 addition and six keyed permutations of A–Z, which preserves the architectural lesson — additive keystream plus per-character permutation, ten coprime-length wheels — while keeping the round-trip clean for arbitrary text.

How It Was Broken
Beurling’s reconstruction (1940)
Initial reconstruction in about two weeks; assistants and later key-recovery work also mattered

FRA publishes Carl-Gösta Borelius’s later reconstruction of how Beurling may have proceeded. Borelius describes messages sent with matching settings, frequent letter-shift and space characters, and regularities preserved when bit addition and permutation act on parallel messages. He explicitly presents a hypothesis, not Beurling’s own step-by-step account. See FRA, “Hur gjorde Arne Beurling för att forcera G-skrivaren?”. Letter shift and space were useful statistical clues within this reconstruction; additive masking still had to be accounted for.

App, the decryption apparatus
Purpose: process traffic after suitable keys were recovered

Beurling and engineer Vigo Lindstein developed decryption equipment known as “appar.” It helped process messages once suitable keys had been recovered; it should not be described as automatically finding every daily setting. This exhibit does not establish that all resulting intelligence was shared with the Allies.

Fish Family Context

"Fish" was Bletchley Park's umbrella codename for German teleprinter systems, named after fish species:

  • Tunny = Lorenz SZ40/42 high-command traffic.
  • Sturgeon = Siemens T52 Geheimschreiber family.
  • Thrasher = the Siemens T43 one-time-tape machine, a separate system.

This matters because the British did not face one "German machine cipher" but a layered ecosystem: Enigma for tactical traffic, Lorenz for OKW strategic command, and T52-class Fish circuits for teleprinter channels.
