Joseph Rochefort

1900 – 1976

Joseph Rochefort was a US Navy codebreaker, linguist, and intelligence officer who commanded Station HYPO — the Combat Intelligence Unit at Pearl Harbor — from early 1941 until his reassignment in 1942. Working in an improvised basement operations center, often sleeping in a bathrobe beside his desk, Rochefort led the team that cracked enough of the Japanese JN-25 additive cipher to name the target of Japan's next major offensive. His intelligence enabled Admiral Nimitz to position three carriers at Midway; the resulting battle sank four Japanese fleet carriers and dealt Japan a major carrier loss in the Pacific.

Why This Person Matters

HYPO’s cryptanalysts, linguists and traffic analysts provided intelligence that helped Admiral Nimitz assess Japanese plans. Their work supported fleet decisions; it was not a single person’s victory. The 1986 posthumous Distinguished Service Medal recognized Rochefort’s contribution after earlier efforts to secure that honor had failed.

The Road to HYPO

Rochefort alternated sea assignments with cryptanalytic, language and intelligence work. Laurance Safford sent him to Hawaii to command Station HYPO in early 1941, before the Pearl Harbor attack. He assembled a team whose analysis of Japanese naval communications became crucial to planning for Midway.

The Midway Intelligence

In May 1942, analysts believed the Japanese designation AF referred to Midway. A false report of a water shortage, devised through discussion involving Rochefort and Jasper Holmes, helped test that identification. NSA’s account identifies a Japanese message translated by FRUMEL on 22 May. This was corroborating intelligence, not an automatic decryption of all JN-25 traffic. Nimitz’s preparations and the subsequent naval battle also depended on reconnaissance, operational decisions and combat.

Injustice and Legacy

Rochefort was reassigned after Midway amid disputes over the intelligence work and its recognition. His later service included a floating-drydock command and leadership of the Pacific Strategic Intelligence Group. On 30 May 1986, President Reagan presented the posthumous Distinguished Service Medal to his family; the Reagan Presidential Library preserves the ceremony record. His removal should not be described as the end of all later intelligence work.
