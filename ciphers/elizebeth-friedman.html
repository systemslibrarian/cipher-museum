Elizebeth Smith Friedman

1892 – 1980

Elizebeth Smith worked at Riverbank Laboratories and married William Friedman. She later led U.S. Coast Guard cryptanalytic work against smugglers and wartime spy communications. Her achievements included training analysts and supplying expert evidence in prosecutions. The wartime work was a team effort, not a single-handed dismantling of every South American spy network.

From a ship’s radio to the witness stand

The Bear Cat, September 1930

As David Kahn recounts in The Codebreakers, chapter 22, the rumrunner Bear Cat drew a pursuing Coast Guard cutter farther out to sea. On 22 September its shore contact advised it to keep going. After the cutter abandoned the pursuit the next day, the smuggler returned to its rendezvous. Reading that exchange afterwards exposed the tactic; reading it in time could have supported a different decision. This episode is a failed opportunity to intervene, not a claim that decipherment sank the ship.

New Orleans, May 1933

Coast Guard listeners at Mobile intercepted Consolidated Exporters Corporation’s radio traffic and sent it to Friedman’s unit in Washington. In the trial beginning 1 May 1933, Friedman explained her decipherments under challenge from the defence. Readable traffic had become evidence that needed to withstand examination.

Source: Patrick D. Weadon, “The Last Line of Defense,” especially “A New Kind of Detective Work”; Kahn, chapter 22, as cited in the book’s chapter 8 notes. For a claimed message-to-person link, ask what a vessel log, interception record or independent witness would establish. The reading alone cannot answer every question about who acted.

Riverbank: a reading must survive a test

Elizebeth Smith arrived at George Fabyan’s Riverbank Laboratories in 1916, where a project sought Baconian messages in Shakespeare. She and William Friedman developed practical cryptanalytic work there; their later The Shakespearean Ciphers Examined (1957) challenged claimed decipherments. Finding a possible pattern and establishing an intended message are different tasks. Sources: the Marshall Foundation’s account of Riverbank and the Friedmans’ 1957 book, also discussed in chapter 7’s notes.

Open the evidence workbench. Record a proposed reading, its rule and an observation that could disprove it. Could someone apply the same rule without being told the answer?

Why This Person Matters

Friedman worked within government institutions, including the Coast Guard and Treasury. Secrecy and uneven attribution obscured parts of her contribution, but it was documented before the 2017 biography. For example, the NSA recognized her in its Cryptologic Hall of Honor and named a building for both Friedmans in 2002. See the NSA biography.

Rum-Running and the Coast Guard

The Coast Guard credits its cryptanalytic operation with deciphering approximately 12,000 smuggling messages. Friedman also testified in criminal cases. Messages, distinct cipher systems, codebooks and prosecutions are different counts. See the Coast Guard’s historical account.

Nazi Spy Networks

Friedman’s wartime unit analyzed clandestine Axis communications, including systems used in South America. This work combined analysts, interception, and interagency cooperation. Cryptanalytic intelligence supported counterintelligence investigations; recovering messages and arresting agents were distinct contributions.

William and Elizebeth

William Friedman led the Army’s Signal Intelligence Service; PURPLE was solved by its team, including Frank Rowlett and Genevieve Grotjan, not by William alone. Elizebeth had a distinct career with her own collaborators. Their contributions can be recognized without inferring medical causes, private motives, or a deliberate design of every official record.
