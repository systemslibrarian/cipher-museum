Why This Matters

The Voynich manuscript contains an undeciphered script and distinctive illustrations. Its parchment dates to the early fifteenth century, but no generally accepted reading establishes its language or whether it is an encrypted text. Failed proposals are not evidence that every possible interpretation is impossible.

Historical Context

A 1639 letter from Georg Baresch provides an early written description of the manuscript; the story that Emperor Rudolf II paid 600 gold ducats for it is later hearsay. Its provenance trail then runs through the alchemist Georg Baresch, the polymath Athanasius Kircher (who failed to decipher it), and a Jesuit library in Rome, where the rare-book dealer Wilfrid Voynich purchased it in 1912. He gave it the name it bears today. Yale's Beinecke Library has held it since 1969 as MS 408.

Radiocarbon dating places the sampled parchment in 1404–1438 at the stated confidence level. This dates the animal material, not directly the writing or ink. The result constrains provenance and is consistent with a medieval manuscript, but should not be described as an exact scientific date for every stage of its production.

William Friedman organized a manuscript study group in 1944 and later proposed a constructed-language explanation. His 1959 anagram recorded a hypothesis, not a decipherment. Mary E. D’Imperio’s The Voynich Manuscript: An Elegant Enigma (1978, §§5.1–5.2 and 6.2–6.6) reviews these earlier investigations. Sravana Reddy and Kevin Knight’s What We Know About The Voynich Manuscript (2011) warns against treating a narrow word-length distribution as proof against natural language: some comparison languages and representations show similar patterns.

💡

Mary D’Imperio’s The Voynich Manuscript: An Elegant Enigma (1978) surveys evidence and competing explanations. Its competing hypotheses should be distinguished from established readings.

What We Actually Know

Measurements depend on transcription, page selection and the comparison corpus. They constrain hypotheses without identifying a unique language or cipher:

  • Glyph alphabet: The inventory depends on how marks and ligatures are divided into symbols. EVA is a transcription convention, not a decipherment.
  • Word length: A narrow distribution is not unique to Voynichese; the choice of writing system and tokenization matters.
  • Conditional entropy: Some letter sequences are highly predictable. The table below reports one specific comparison, not a theorem about every natural language.
  • Section dialects: Currier A and B label statistical groupings. They do not establish exactly two scribes, languages or encryption keys.
  • Word repetition: Repeated and near-repeated tokens provide evidence about structure; their cause is unresolved.
  • Page-position effects: A mark’s frequency can depend on its location within words or lines.
Selected Proposed Interpretations
William Newbold (1921)
Status: Historical claim rejected

Newbold attributed microscopic shorthand to Roger Bacon. D’Imperio’s §5.1 explains John Manly’s objections: the supposed tiny signs were cracks in ink, and flexible substitutions and anagrams allowed incompatible readings.

Joseph Martin Feely (1943)
Status: Historical claim rejected

Feely tried a substitution into abbreviated Latin using published manuscript images. D’Imperio’s §5.2 reports that his proposed Latin and abbreviation practices were not accepted as a valid reading.

William Friedman’s constructed-language hypothesis
Status: Hypothesis, not a decipherment

Friedman explored artificial languages as possible analogues. His study group and 1959 anagram did not establish the manuscript’s language or recover its text.

Stephen Bax (2014)
Status: Proposed partial reading

In A Proposed Partial Decoding of the Voynich Script, Bax proposed ten words and fourteen signs or clusters, using possible plant and constellation names. These proposed correspondences are not a demonstrated translation of the manuscript.

Gerard Cheshire (2019)
Status: Disputed interpretation

Cheshire proposed a “proto-Romance” reading. In its statement of 16 May 2019, the University of Bristol said it had removed its news story after specialists raised concerns and while it sought further validation. Removing publicity is distinct from retracting a journal article.

⚠️

How to evaluate a claimed reading: A claimed decipherment must (a) work consistently across the whole manuscript, not just cherry-picked words; (b) produce grammatically coherent text in the claimed language; (c) explain observed statistical features without assuming they uniquely identify a language. These are evaluation criteria, not a proof that decipherment is impossible. No generally accepted decipherment is established here.

Evidence and Reading

Sravana Reddy and Kevin Knight, “What We Know About The Voynich Manuscript” (2011), especially §§4.1–4.2 and Table 2. Their §2.2 specifies 225 text-bearing pages and 37,919 word tokens in their transcription; these are not invariant counts for every transcription. These measurements are not a decipherment. Mary D’Imperio, The Voynich Manuscript: An Elegant Enigma (1978), is a historical survey of the problem. Yale’s physical catalogue records 102 parchment leaves with foldouts, which should not be confused with a count of unfolded page surfaces. Greg Hodgins’s Dating Results for Voynich Manuscript Parchment Leaves 8, 26, 47 and 68 (17 February 2009), pp.1–3 and Figure 7, reports the combined calibrated range; the report explicitly distinguishes parchment dating from the date of writing.

About this Demo

The interactive demo is an invented A–Z substitution using ordinary Latin letters. It is not an EVA transcription of the manuscript, a rendering of its glyphs or a proposed decipherment. It preserves the input’s word lengths and spaces, so its output does not reproduce Voynich word structure.

EVA, developed by Gabriel Landini and René Zandbergen with contributions from Jacques Guy, represents shapes in manuscript transcriptions. A transcription records visible marks; it does not translate them. The historical object is cataloged as Voynich Manuscript, Beinecke MS 408, Yale University.

If You Want to Try

To investigate a proposed reading:

  • Read Mary E. D’Imperio’s The Voynich Manuscript: An Elegant Enigma (1978).
  • Use a documented transcription and record ambiguous glyphs and any changes.
  • Test your hypothesis on the whole manuscript, not 10 cherry-picked words.
  • Explain how the proposed process generates the observed transcription, including statistical patterns.
  • Publish a reproducible method and seek independent testing and scholarly criticism.
