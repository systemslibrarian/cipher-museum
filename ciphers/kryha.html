Why This Matters

Alexander von Kryha probably introduced the Standard in Berlin in 1924. He later fitted the same cipher into the pocket-watch-sized Liliput. Kryha advertised to businesses and displayed his machines at trade shows. Historian Klaus Schmeh identified military customers in Spain and the Soviet Union, but could establish no prewar sales total. Schmeh, pp. 292–294.

How It Works

The Standard had a stationary semicircle for the plaintext and a rotating disk for the ciphertext. The operator could rearrange the letter tabs on both. After reading off each letter, the operator pressed a lever to advance the disk. On the early selector described by Lambros Callimahos, 17 successive steps moved the disk a total of 111 places. That left the alphabets seven letters apart from their starting alignment. Both the selector and the alphabets returned to their starting positions after 442 letters. Callimahos, appendix, pp. 20–21.

The museum demo uses its seed word to generate one mixed alphabet and 26 step sizes from one to six. Its example runs on those teaching settings.

How It Was Broken
Friedman, Kullback, Rowlett and Sinkov, 1933
Reported challenge solution: four analysts, 2 hours 41 minutes

William Friedman, Solomon Kullback, Frank Rowlett and Abraham Sinkov solved a 1,135-character Kryha test message in 2 hours 41 minutes in 1933. The team recovered the alphabets and starting position. The time stamps span 3 hours 31 minutes, including a recorded 50-minute lunch break. Callimahos, pp. 16, 18.

Kasiski / index of coincidence
Depends on the period, text length and available evidence

The repeated stepping structure creates related substitutions that can be exploited. How much ciphertext is needed depends on the settings, message and available information; “a few hundred characters” is not a guaranteed threshold.
