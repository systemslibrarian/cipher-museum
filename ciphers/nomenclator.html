Why This Matters

Nomenclators combined a substitution alphabet with entries for words, names or phrases. They were widely used in European diplomacy over several centuries, but did not form one uniform system used by every court or every famous correspondent. Individual keys must be examined to distinguish substitution, syllabic codes and larger codebooks.

What does one code stand for?

An entry can encode a named entity, number, content word, function word, syllable, morphological ending, phrase, sentence or punctuation. These linguistic categories differ from operational null, repeat and erase instructions. The invented key 21 → seal; 22 → ed; 23 → . reads 21 22 23 as sealed.: an ending and punctuation, neither treated as an alphabet letter. Load and apply this example in the evidence workbench.

The category must come from the key or other evidence, not its century or frequency. The archival sample in What Was Encoded in Historical Cipher Keys in the Early Modern Era?, pp. 2–8 (examples pp. 3–4), is uneven and often lacks origin/use information; some chart counts disagree with nearby prose. It does not establish a universal diplomatic trend. The workbench's unknown-key solver remains restricted to letter substitutions.

Historical Context

Kahn describes Gabrieli di Lavinde’s 1379 collection of keys combining letter substitutions with short word and name lists. Mantua’s 1401 key is a distinct landmark in Western homophonic substitution, not the beginning of nomenclators. Later diplomatic keys varied greatly in size and structure; Black Chambers reconstructed some of them from traffic and context.

How It Works

A teaching example can combine three parts: (1) a substitution alphabet mapping each letter to one or more cipher symbols (often digits 01–99), (2) a vocabulary list of perhaps 1,500 common words and names mapped to higher numbers, and (3) a list of nulls — symbols that mean ‘ignore me’ — sprinkled in to break frequency. The following invented entries illustrate changing granularity: The ENVOY arrived in VIENNA might come out as 23 1402 41 17 1899.

How It Was Broken
Black-Chamber Reconstruction
Complexity: Effort varies by key and available evidence

Substitution entries may yield to letter and word patterns, while code groups often need repeated contexts and external historical evidence. Success varies by system and available traffic. No general claim that a Black Chamber broke every replacement key within months is established here.
