Two versions, two ways of obtaining evidence

Follow the two legs separately. Re-encoding created a different numerical text of the same proposal; obtaining that second text also gave Hall a way to disclose the proposal while shielding the earlier access.

1 · Berlin to Washington — code 0075

Zimmermann’s instruction went to Ambassador Bernstorff in Washington over transatlantic communications. Britain intercepted traffic passing through its cable access; Room 40 worked on the newer code 0075. This was the sensitive access Hall wanted to protect.

2 · Washington to Mexico — code 13040

Bernstorff’s office forwarded the proposal to the German minister in Mexico, re-encoded in the older code 13040. British agents obtained a copy from the telegraph system in Mexico and supplied it to Room 40. This was acquisition of a forwarded copy, not a second interception at the same British cable point.

What could be shown?

The Mexican version provided both more readable code groups and a plausible source for disclosure. Comparing a translation with a commercial telegram record could help establish authenticity without explaining every British interception.

Sources: David Kahn, The Codebreakers, chapter 9 (the book’s chapter 4 notes); Jay Bellamy, “The Zimmermann Telegram,” National Archives. This route view follows those accounts; it does not claim a single physical path for every transmitted copy.

Why This Matters

On 16 January 1917, Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann instructed Germany’s ambassador in Washington to propose an alliance with Mexico if the United States entered the war. The terms included German support for Mexico to recover Texas, New Mexico and Arizona. British interception and decipherment exposed the proposal. Its publication on 1 March increased pressure for US entry into the war, alongside Germany’s unrestricted submarine campaign and other events.

Britain also needed to protect its intelligence sources. Ambassador Walter Page’s telegram of 24 February records the text received from Britain and the account that copies had been bought in Mexico. That contemporary diplomatic account can be compared with the later declassified cryptologic history.

How It Works

The Berlin-to-Washington transmission used diplomatic code 0075. Washington re-encoded the onward message to Mexico in the older code 13040 because the Mexican embassy did not hold 0075. Room 40 had recovered much more of the older code. A copy obtained in Mexico helped fill gaps in the reading and gave Britain a way to disclose the telegram without identifying the initial interception source.

The two code numbers describe successive transmission legs, not an additive layer applied to one ciphertext. The museum widget separately demonstrates a small codebook with additive superencipherment; that added operation is a teaching choice, not a reconstruction of this telegram’s procedure.

The widget has a fixed table of 126 entries (123 distinct words) and sentinel-marked letter fallback for other words. Its entries, arithmetic and fallback conventions are modern examples.

How It Was Broken
Diplomatic codebook reconstruction
Accumulated traffic and partial code recovery

The 0075 reading was incomplete when the telegram arrived. The more extensively recovered 13040 version helped resolve missing passages. Captures of German naval codebooks aided other Room 40 work; they are not the diplomatic codebooks used for this telegram.

Hall’s deception operation
Complexity: Statecraft, not cryptanalysis

Admiral Sir William “Blinker” Hall, head of Room 40, used a British agent in Mexico to obtain a copy of the cable from a commercial telegraph office in Mexico City — an entirely real document that allowed the British to share the plaintext with the Americans while concealing the initial interception route and Room 40’s role.
