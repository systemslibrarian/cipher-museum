Why This Matters

The researchers initially encountered unidentified ciphertexts among digitised manuscripts at the Bibliothèque nationale de France. Decipherment revealed letters written by Mary Stuart in 1578–1584, mostly to the French ambassador Michel de Castelnau. Their identification was part of the discovery: the documents were not already correctly catalogued as this correspondence.

The Cipher System: What Mary Used

Mary's Castelnau cipher is a sophisticated Renaissance nomenclator — the same class of system used in the Babington Plot correspondence (1586), which was broken by Thomas Phelippes. The Castelnau cipher is substantially more complex:

  • Symbol alphabet: 219 distinct graphical symbol types identified across the corpus, including homophones, nomenclature and special signs. Diacritics can change a symbol’s meaning; this is not a 219-letter alphabet.
  • Code groups: Named persons, places, and frequent political terms replaced by dedicated codewords (e.g., a specific symbol for "Monsieur de Castelnau," another for "Her Majesty the Queen of England").
  • Nulls: Non-semantic symbols interspersed to inflate symbol frequency counts and frustrate statistical attacks.
  • Special symbols: The recovered system also includes signs that modify how neighbouring symbols are read. The paper documents their functions; this is not evidence of a separate double-encryption layer.

The plaintext language is French — an additional barrier for analysts who might expect English or Latin in Mary's correspondence.

How the Break Worked: Hill-Climbing at Scale

The attack method, developed by George Lasry, is a variant of the simulated annealing / hill-climbing approach used in modern historical cryptanalysis. The key components:

  1. Transcription: The team converted the handwritten ciphertext symbols into machine-readable identifiers. The approximately 50,000 figure describes plaintext words, not cipher characters.
  2. Revised hypotheses: An initial Italian-language hypothesis gave way to French. Diacritic and symbol assignments were corrected as decipherment progressed.
  3. Homophone search: The first search provisionally treated symbols as letters. Simulated annealing changed mappings and sometimes accepted worse scores to escape local optima, using historical French language statistics.
  4. Computer-assisted search: Candidate substitutions were evaluated using language statistics; human analysis then resolved symbols, words and context that the initial output did not explain.
  5. Manual refinement and corroboration: Lasry, Biermann, and Tomokiyo recovered nomenclature for words, syllables, names and places manually, revised transcription choices, and compared surviving plaintext versions. The search did not automatically recover the complete nomenclator.

Computer-assisted cryptanalysis and archival interpretation complemented one another. Readable fragments helped identify the writer, after which historical context helped refine the decipherment.

What the Letters Say

The full content of the deciphered letters is reported in the Cryptologia paper and its supplementary materials. Key categories of newly revealed content include:

  • Captivity conditions: Mary describes her treatment in captivity, her access to correspondence and to her attendants, and her physical health — primary-source details not previously available from the cipher side of the archive.
  • Political strategy: Mary outlines her diplomatic positioning — appealing to the French crown for intervention, requesting Castelnau relay messages to specific parties at the French court, and describing her view of her prospects in England.
  • Undocumented intermediaries: The letters name individuals not previously known to have been involved in Mary's communications network, expanding the known cast of her diplomatic circle.
  • Assessment of Walsingham: Mary's own characterisation of Sir Francis Walsingham and her awareness of surveillance — a rare first-person account of Tudor intelligence from the surveilled rather than the surveiller.
💡

Historical significance: The decipherment makes approximately 50,000 words of correspondence available for historical study. Some letters had other surviving versions; the recovery should not be described as an entirely unknown archive in every detail.

The Solvers

George Lasry — Israeli cryptanalyst; built the hill-climbing toolkit used across multiple historical cipher solutions; Cryptologia contributor.

Norbert Biermann — Pianist, musical coach and arranger; Professor of Repertoire at the Berlin University of Arts in the paper’s 2023 author biography; historical cryptology researcher.

Satoshi Tomokiyo — Independent researcher; specialist in Renaissance and early-modern European diplomatic cipher manuscripts; extensive bibliography on Argenti-family systems.
