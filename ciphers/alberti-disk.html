Why This Matters

Leon Battista Alberti’s 1467 cipher disk was an early documented polyalphabetic system and an important cipher device . His description explains how changing alignment lets letters take different ciphertext values within one message.

Historical Context

Leon Battista Alberti — architect, artist, humanist, and one of the great Renaissance polymaths — invented the cipher disk in 1467 and described it in his treatise De Cifris. It is an early documented European polyalphabetic design; calling it the first mechanical cipher device would overlook earlier devices such as the scytale.

Alberti described concentric disks with different alphabets. His historical outer ring included 20 letters and the digits 1–4; it was not the ordinary modern 26-letter alphabet. Changing their relative alignment changes the substitution. The diagram and widget here are simplified teaching representations, not an exact reproduction of the historical rings.

How It Works
Teaching model: two 26-letter rings (setting 0)
Outer: ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ
Inner: AZBYCXDWEVFUGTHSIRJQKPLOMN

At setting 0: A → A, B → Z, C → B.
The widget adds its initial setting to each outer index.
After every four processed letters, the setting increases by one.

This fixed four-letter schedule and these rings are modern
teaching choices. Alberti’s 24-cell disk used different rings
and communicated changes of alignment in the message.

How It Was Broken
Index of Coincidence
Complexity: Easy-Moderate

Irregular alphabet changes avoid a simple fixed keyword period. If enough text shares an alignment, language statistics and probable words may help recover that portion. The index of coincidence does not automatically identify every boundary or guarantee recovery of every alignment from an arbitrary sample.
