The interactive is a deterministic teaching model: a seed generates all six pin patterns and all 27 lug bars. It demonstrates the pin-and-lug mechanism but is not an operational key-sheet simulator.

Why This Matters

Boris Hagelin's C-38 design, sold to the US Army as the M-209, became the standard tactical cipher of American forces in WWII. Compact (about the size of a hardcover book), it weighed 2.7 kg and ran without electricity — a clerk operated it by hand, selecting each input letter and turning a crank to print the output on paper tape. The large production contract made the machine a major commercial success.

Historical Context

Hagelin’s cipher-machine business developed the C-series before the war. David Kahn distinguishes the 1940 US trials and negotiations from production beginning in 1942; Kahn and George Lasry report approximately 140,000 machines. The US-produced M-209, associated with the C-38 family, became a widely used portable tactical machine. It offered practical field operation without electricity, but published cryptanalysis shows why its mechanical complexity should not be mistaken for modern security.

How It Works

Six pinwheels with co-prime lengths (26, 25, 23, 21, 19, 17) advance one position per letter. Each wheel has tiny pins around its edge, set to "active" or "inactive" by the operator. A bank of 27 lug bars reads which wheels are currently active and produces a displacement K from 0 to 27. The indicator alphabet is reversed, so with A=0 encryption uses the reciprocal transformation:

cipher = (25 - plaintext + K) mod 26
With no displaced bars A becomes Z; with 20 displaced bars A becomes T. Because the wheel lengths are pairwise co-prime, the joint wheel positions repeat after 26 × 25 × 23 × 21 × 19 × 17 = 101,405,850 steps. The output keystream can have a smaller period, depending on pin and lug settings.

How It Was Broken
Known-Plaintext Attack
Work depends on message length, pin settings and lug overlap

Known or accurately guessed plaintext exposes the displacement sequence modulo 26 and constrains pin and lug settings. Published attacks exploit this structure, but their cost and success depend on available text and key restrictions. A fixed “hours per message” claim should not be applied to arbitrary historical traffic.

Statistical Lug Recovery
Statistical search under specified key and traffic conditions

Each lug bar contributes one unit if at least one of the pins selected by its lugs is effective. If both selected pins are effective, that bar still contributes only one unit. The total is therefore a sum of bar-level OR results, not simply a separate lug count times each wheel’s pin bit. Lug overlaps affect the distribution and the difficulty of reconstruction; statistics can guide search without guaranteeing unique recovery.

Hagelin Machine Family

See the Hagelin C-36 / C-52 lineage exhibit for the family and Crypto AG history. The M-209 belongs to the broader Hagelin line (C-36, C-38/BC-38, and M-209). These pin-and-lug pocket machines traded top-tier security for field practicality and became the standard tactical wheel ciphers for multiple armies.

Later Hagelin successors became part of the Crypto AG ecosystem connected to Operation Rubicon, where intelligence services reportedly leveraged supply-chain influence for cryptanalytic advantage.
