Two Soviet messages were enciphered with the same one-time pad segment. Enter a guessed crib and drag it across positions; the pad cancels and reveals candidate fragments of the other plaintext.

Why This Matters
VENONA demonstrates the consequences of duplicated one-time-pad material. American and British analysts worked on a fixed body of intercepted Soviet traffic, chiefly from the 1940s. The project continued from 1943 to 1980; that is not forty years of continuous decryption of newly sent Soviet messages.

    
The Key Reuse Disaster
VENONA began on 1 February 1943, with Gene Grabeel starting the examination of accumulated Soviet traffic. Later that year, Richard Hallock’s work on trade messages helped expose duplicated additive material. In Soviet intelligence systems, analysts had to remove the repeated additive layer and then reconstruct the underlying numerical codebook. Robert L. Benson’s The VENONA Story attributes the opening to apparently duplicated pad pages at the manufacturing centre. Recovery was partial and often took years; this was not immediate reading of every message.

    
How the Break Worked
The cryptanalytic attack exploited "depth" — messages enciphered with repeated pad material. In the decimal model above, subtracting the ciphertext groups digit by digit modulo 10 cancels the shared pad: C₁ − C₂ = G₁ − G₂. That gives a relationship between two initially unknown code groups, not an automatic recovery of either one. Known or guessed groups, patterns across traffic, and further evidence could help reconstruct additive material; recovering codebook meanings was a separate task. The XOR panel shows the analogous cancellation in binary, not the historical traffic format.

    
The Revelations
VENONA provided important leads concerning Soviet espionage, including the atomic network and Donald Maclean, associated with the cover name HOMER. Identifications required intelligence and investigative corroboration. It did not simply identify all five members of the Cambridge spy group, and it should not be treated as a substitute for examining the evidence concerning each individual.

    
From Wartime Analysis to Public Release
VENONA began in 1943, ended as an active project in 1980, and entered public release in 1995. Those are three different dates: thirty-seven years describes the project’s operation, not its entire period of secrecy. Evaluating the political and investigative consequences requires case-specific evidence; the decrypts should not be treated as support for every contemporary accusation.

        SIGTOT and 5-UCO — Allied One-Time Tape Systems
      

      

        
SIGTOT was an American one-time-tape teleprinter system. The 1953 UK/US communications-security assessment made its approval for offline use conditional on checking key-tape quality and physically preventing tape reuse. That assessment describes requirements; it does not demonstrate flawless handling in every deployment.

        
Britain’s 5-UCO was an electronic one-time-tape system for teleprinter communications. Its operation depended on synchronising the sender’s and receiver’s key tape. As with SIGTOT, the mathematical principle and the implementation’s operational security need separate assessment.

        
Parallel lesson: One-time-tape systems such as SIGTOT and 5-UCO share the same dependence on secret, random, non-reused key material. VENONA demonstrates an operational failure in particular Soviet traffic, not a proof that every allied system or implementation resisted every attack.

        
References: David Kahn, The Codebreakers: The Comprehensive History of Secret Communication from Ancient Times to the Internet (revised edition, 1996); UK/US Communications Security Conference 1953: Report of the Security Sub-Committee to the Executive Committee, Appendix A, p.2 (SIGTOT offline assessment); Robert L. Benson, The VENONA Story, pp.1–3 and 26–28; Crypto Museum’s examined-hardware account of 5-UCO.

The people behind the recoveries
Gene Grabeel’s initial sorting and Richard Hallock’s analysis were beginnings, not an instant solution. Cecil Phillips identified “reverse depths” in 1945: some clerks used a page of additive forwards and then backwards. Meredith Gardner later reconstructed codebook meanings; his December 1946 work exposed a list of scientists connected with atomic research.
William Weisband’s access to the work and his Soviet espionage introduce a different question: who could learn what the readers themselves knew? The history of compromise is separate from the arithmetic demonstration. A recovered message still required interpretation and corroboration.
Read Robert L. Benson, The VENONA Story, and Stephen Budiansky, Code Warriors (2016), particularly the early account of the Soviet traffic and Gardner’s 1946 recoveries. The book’s chapter 14 source notes identify the passages used.
