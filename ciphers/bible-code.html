Bible Code — Equidistant Letter Sequences

A lesson in probability: ELS counterexamples and experimental flexibility challenge claims of hidden prophecies.

Statistical claims challenged by controlled counterexamples and analysis of selection flexibility.

Claimed byWitztum, Rips & Rosenberg (1994); Michael Drosnin (1997)
MethodEquidistant Letter Sequences (ELS) in Hebrew Torah text
Refuted byMcKay, Bar-Natan, Bar-Hillel & Kalai — Statistical Science 14:150 (1999)
Modern LessonSearching many alternatives creates opportunities for chance matches; significance depends on the test and selection process

Why This Exhibit Exists

This exhibit concerns a disputed claim of hidden messages, not a demonstrated encryption system. It separates the 1994 statistical experiment on Genesis from later popular predictions, and uses ELS searches to explain why a pattern’s existence alone does not establish intent or statistical significance.

Historical Context

In 1994 Israeli mathematicians Doron Witztum, Eliyahu Rips, and Yoav Rosenberg published a paper in Statistical Science claiming to find statistically significant equidistant letter sequences (ELS) in the Hebrew text of Genesis. They claimed that names of famous rabbis and associated dates occurred unusually close together under their chosen ELS proximity measure.

The paper caused a sensation. In 1997 journalist Michael Drosnin published The Bible Code, extending the method to predict modern events — the assassination of Yitzhak Rabin (cited after the fact), the Gulf War, and others. Drosnin's challenge: "When my critics find a message about the assassination of a prime minister encrypted in Moby-Dick, I'll believe them."

Brendan McKay published Moby-Dick assassination-name examples in response to Drosnin’s popular challenge. This was distinct from the 1999 statistical paper by McKay, Bar-Natan, Bar-Hillel and Kalai, which examined the Genesis experiment’s data and design choices and reported unsuccessful new tests. Neither finding means that every text has the same matches.

How ELS Works

Take any text. Remove all spaces, punctuation, and numbers, keeping only letters. This gives you a letter string. Now pick a target word and a skip value d. Starting at position p, read every d-th letter: positions p, p+d, p+2d, and so on. If the result spells your target word, you have found an ELS at (start=p, skip=d).

Text = ABCDEFGHI, target = ADG
Zero-based start 0, positive skip 3: positions 0, 3, 6.

For length N, target length m, and positive skips 1…D:
valid start/skip pairs = Σ max(N − (m−1)d, 0).

N = 100,000, m = 4, D = 1,000:
98,498,500 valid pairs (not 100,000,000).
Under independent uniformly distributed A–Z letters,
expected hits = 98,498,500 / 26⁴ ≈ 215.54.

The expectation above uses an independent uniform-letter model, not real prose. With independently sampled nonuniform letters, multiply the number of valid pairs by the product of the target letters’ probabilities. Overlapping searches need not be independent for this expectation, but a significance test needs an appropriate distribution and must account for other searched words and settings.

How the Claim Was Refuted
Statistical Replication Test (McKay et al., 1999)
Method: Examine experimental flexibility and attempt independent tests

The 1999 paper examines choices in the Genesis experiment. Its §6 demonstrates how changing permissible rabbinical names and spellings can produce a strong-looking result in a Hebrew War and Peace passage while weakening the Genesis result. It is a demonstration of selection flexibility, not an identical search using an unchanged list in both books. The separate Moby-Dick examples address Drosnin’s popular interpretation.

The Multiplicity Problem (searching for significance)
Method: Combinatorial argument

Trying more names, spellings, skips, directions, and texts creates more opportunities for matches. Hits are not certain for every target, and no single search-space count applies without specifying those choices. Exploratory discoveries need independent confirmation or a statistical method that accounts for the selection process.

What This Teaches Modern Cryptography
Mistake in the Bible Code claimModern cryptographic parallel
Searching billions of (start, skip) pairs post-hoc and reporting only the hits Statistical significance in security audits: under valid null hypotheses, 1,000 tests at a 0.05 threshold have an expected 50 false positives; the observed count need not be exactly 50
Treating a pattern found in scripture as non-accidental without comparing to a random baseline Distinguisher attacks: a valid linear or differential distinguisher requires showing the bias in the cipher is greater than expected from a random permutation, not merely that a bias exists
Unaccounted flexibility in selecting names, spellings and tests can change reported significance Chosen-input bias: choosing a dataset after inspecting results can invalidate a test’s stated significance
Short texts and flexible interpretation can permit competing readings State the cipher model and constraints before judging a proposed decipherment; a plausible phrase alone does not identify the original message
Publication and independent examination can reach different conclusions Security proofs require adversarial review: a published claim still needs independent examination of its assumptions, evidence, and reproducibility; publication alone does not settle it
⚠

On the original 1994 paper. The criticism concerns the experimental choices and the interpretation of the evidence. It does not establish the authors’ motives. Multiple-comparison problems were known before 1994; the important question is whether the reported procedure accounted adequately for its choices and search flexibility.

The Numbers

For an illustrative text of 78,000 letters, a five-letter target, and positive skips 1–1,000, there are 75,998,000 valid start/skip pairs. Compare two explicitly idealized alphabets:

75,998,000 start × skip pairs searched
1 in 26⁵ = 1 in 11.9M chance per pair
~6.40 expected hits: independent uniform A–Z model
~14.75 expected hits: independent uniform 22-letter model (N / 22⁵)

These expectations are for artificial independent-letter models. Hebrew has 22 base letters, not 26; its real letter frequencies and textual structure are not uniform. A number of hits alone cannot establish significance, and a 78,000-letter example should not be mislabeled as the complete Torah.
