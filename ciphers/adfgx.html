ADFGX Cipher

Broken

A German field cipher attacked through relationships among wartime messages

Painvin and other Allied analysts used repeated traffic, statistics and transposition analysis in 1918.

InventorLieutenant Fritz Nebel · German Army
YearMarch 1918
Key TypePolybius key square + columnar key
Broken ByGeorges Painvin and Allied analysts · 1918
Modern LessonCompare fractionation and transposition with modern round structures

Why This Matters

ADFGX combined a coordinate substitution with columnar transposition. Its analysis during the 1918 offensives illustrates how related messages and operational procedures can assist an attack on a layered cipher. It does not establish that one analyst alone determined the war’s outcome.

Historical Context

As Germany prepared its 1918 Spring Offensive, Fritz Nebel designed the ADFGX cipher for front-line use. The letters A, D, F, G, X were specifically chosen because they sound very distinct in Morse code — reducing the risk of garbled transmissions misreading one letter as another.

Georges Painvin solved important ADFGX traffic in 1918 and then attacked its six-symbol extension, ADFGVX. Recovering particular keys required suitable intercepted material and sustained analysis; it was not a guaranteed solution to every message.

How It Works
Step 1: keyed 5×5 square, keyword PRIVACY (I/J share a cell)
          A D F G X
        A P R I V A
        D C Y B D E
        F F G H K L
        G M N O Q S
        X T U W X Z

        Coordinates are row, then column.
        Each letter → pair from {A,D,F,G,X}
        H → FF   E → DX   L → FX

Step 2: Columnar transposition with keyword
        Write pairs in rows, read by column order

HELLO → FF DX FX FX GF
Column key: G E R M A N
            F F D X F X
            F X G F
Read A,E,G,M,N,R columns → F FX FF XF X DG
Final ciphertext: FFXFFXFXDG
How It Was Broken
Painvin’s analysis of related traffic (1918)
Complexity: Hard · Broken under wartime pressure

Painvin used relationships among intercepted messages and hypotheses about their structure to recover keys. The work required suitable traffic, not a guaranteed attack from one short message. His own later account distinguishes particular recovered keys from a universal break of all ADFGX traffic.
