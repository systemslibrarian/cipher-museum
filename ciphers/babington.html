The delivery route and the second reader

For an outgoing letter, follow the household’s message into the brewer’s cask and the channel operated through Gilbert Gifford. That covert delivery channel was controlled by Walsingham’s network. Thomas Phelippes could copy and decipher intercepted correspondence; intelligence reached Walsingham while the delivery channel continued to operate.

  1. Delivery: Mary’s household; the brewer’s cask; Gifford’s channel; onward delivery to the correspondent.
  2. Access to the contents: interception within that channel; Phelippes’s copying and decipherment; a reading for Walsingham.

These are connected routes, not a claim that Walsingham personally carried each original to its destination. Ask where a copy could leave the delivery chain without stopping the letter.

Sources: Simon Singh, The Code Book, chapter 1, cited in the book’s chapter 1 notes; Alan Bryson, “The Gallows Letter,” British Library, on interception and Phelippes’s decipherment of Mary’s 17 July 1586 letter.

Why This Matters

In 1586 Mary, Queen of Scots, was held at Chartley. Walsingham’s agents intercepted her encrypted correspondence with Anthony Babington. The letters became evidence at her trial, followed by her execution on February 8, 1587. Cryptanalysis mattered, alongside interception, secretarial testimony, and the political and judicial process; it was not the sole cause of the outcome.

Historical Context

By 1586, Mary had been held in England for about eighteen years. Walsingham sought evidence of her involvement in plots against Elizabeth. The correspondence passed through secretaries and couriers; it should not be described as necessarily written in Mary’s own hand.

Gilbert Gifford helped establish a channel that Mary’s correspondents believed private, but Walsingham’s agents could intercept and decipher its letters. The historical system combined letter symbols, codewords, and special signs. The exact 64-token inventory used below belongs to the museum’s model, not a verified transcription of the original key.

Phelippes deciphered Mary’s July 17 letter and added a postscript requesting the conspirators’ names. No reply supplying those names is established by this account. Walsingham’s August 3 letter worried that the postscript had made Babington suspicious. See the British Library’s discussion of the surviving documents.

💡

The deciphered correspondence was used against Mary, who disputed the accusations. A dramatic statement about her reaction is not a substitute for the surviving trial and correspondence records.

How It Works

The cipher was a nomenclator, combining substitution with a codebook. The following inventory describes the museum’s teaching model; its token counts and assignments are not an archival facsimile:

23 letter symbols   (no J, V, W — folded onto I, U, UU)
36 codewords        (THE, AND, OF, MARY, QUEEN, ELIZABETH, POISON, …)
 4 null symbols     (decoys — drop on decode)
 1 "doubleth"       (means: double the previous/next letter)
─────────
64 total glyphs

This software uses a greedy-longest-match scan: it selects a codeword where possible, otherwise a letter token. It can insert nulls and abbreviate doubled letters. These are the model’s deterministic rules, not a documented reconstruction of a clerk’s exact working procedure.

How It Was Broken
Frequency Analysis on the Letter Glyphs
Complexity: Moderate · Took Phelippes days, not weeks

Letter substitution leaks repeated patterns and language statistics; codewords and nulls complicate analysis. Frequency counts supply hypotheses, not a guaranteed E–T mapping or a complete reconstruction of Phelippes’s method. The surviving evidence establishes his successful decipherment, not the precise workflow previously asserted here.

Ciphertext Forgery (Active Attack)
A documented forged-postscript episode

Knowledge of an enciphering key lets an attacker create plausible new ciphertext. Phelippes’s forged postscript illustrates impersonation after key compromise. It is not a formal chosen-ciphertext attack: no decryption-oracle experiment is established, and the requested reply is not known to have arrived.

Operational Compromise (Channel Capture)
Complexity: Trivial once the courier turned

Control of the delivery channel enabled interception and alteration. Modern end-to-end encryption can protect messages over an adversary-controlled transport when endpoint keys and authentication remain secure. Compromising an endpoint or its secret key is a different and more powerful attack.

⚠️

Two distinct failures: the historical cipher was deciphered, and possession of its enciphering knowledge enabled a forged message. Adding codewords does not erase language structure. Authentication can resist outsider forgery only while the relevant authentication key remains secret.

🎓

Modern comparison, not a claimed direct lineage: authenticated encryption checks message integrity as well as confidentiality. It does not prevent an attacker who has stolen the shared authentication key from generating valid messages. Nor can a modern primitive settle the historical counterfactual about Mary’s trial.

About this Demo

The original cipher chart survives in The National Archives (SP 12/193). This demo uses ASCII tokens drawn from a 64-symbol pool — ⟨a01⟩…⟨a23⟩, ⟨w01⟩…⟨w36⟩, ⟨n01⟩…⟨n04⟩, and ⟨x2⟩ — but the seed shuffles which symbol stands for which letter, codeword, null, or the doubleth, so a symbol's prefix says nothing about its role. Under the default seed BABINGTON, for example, the doubleth is ⟨w18⟩ and ⟨x2⟩ is the codeword DEATH.

Try encoding "POISON THE QUEEN AT THE TOWER" and notice how POISON, THE, QUEEN, and TOWER each get their own codeword (one glyph for the whole word). Then encode "ATTACK" and watch the double-letter abbreviation apply on the doubled T.

The demonstration folds J to I, V to U, and W to UU. These are explicit model conventions, not proof that all Elizabethan cipher alphabets used the same inventory.
