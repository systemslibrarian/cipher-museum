Why This Matters

The Copiale manuscript contains about 75,000 handwritten characters across 105 pages. In 2011 Kevin Knight, Beáta Megyesi, and Christiane Schaefer reported its decipherment using computational analysis and human linguistic reasoning. It contains German text about a secret society’s rituals. Their original paper documents the sequence of hypotheses, including unsuccessful ones.

Historical Context

A crucial discovery was that unaccented Roman letters represented word spaces, while other signs encoded the text. They were not arbitrary meaningless filler. Grouping symbols by their contexts, testing likely words, and examining German spelling helped the researchers work out the system.

How It Works

The manuscript uses homophones, word separators, and signs for some letter groups and concepts. Its roughly 90 cipher signs do not divide uniformly among 26 letters. The common letter E has several representations; unaccented Roman letters mark spaces. The model should not equate every extra symbol with a random null.

How It Was Broken
Computational and Linguistic Analysis (2011)
Complexity: Computational

The initial automated attack on the wrong subset of symbols failed. The published solution combined statistical tools with changes of hypothesis and linguistic interpretation; switching a language setting did not instantly produce the full plaintext.

Hypothesis-Driven Symbol Classification
Complexity: Insight

Recognizing the space symbols and language-dependent groups was essential. A good statistical tool cannot compensate for an incorrect assumption about which symbols carry letters, separators, or other information.
