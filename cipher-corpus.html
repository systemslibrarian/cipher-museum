Cipher Corpus

The corrected v0.8 corpus has 100,313 records for learning, testing, and benchmarking classical cryptanalysis.

Each record includes cipher type, key, plaintext, difficulty, language, and expected attacks. The on-page browser samples v0.8: use Challenge Mode to attempt a solve, or switch to Known Answers to study the metadata. Each card states its pair verification and provenance status; a verified computation does not authenticate a historical source.

The separately published v0.6 archive has 100,326 records. Of these, 99,800 replayed with museum engines and 526 noisy or historical-variant records had documented exceptions. Dataset scope and conventions.

Published archive: Zenodo v0.6 · DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23297920. The archive preserves canonical JSONL plus 11 supporting files; the website also provides derived JSON, CSV, subsets, and browser samples.

Two statistics that are easy to swap

Corpus-level entropy and per-message entropy are different measurements. With 26 plaintext letters and at most five homophones each, one fixed homophonic key has at most 130 code types, so a code symbol under that key carries at most log2 130 ≈ 7.022 bits. Pooling ciphertext produced under many independently generated keys enlarges the observed alphabet — eleven thousand codes gives log2 11000 ≈ 13.425 bits — but that figure describes the collection. It does not describe what an attacker holding one message faces, and a recovery result that improves when the pool grows is measuring the pool rather than the cipher. The entropy exhibit works a small enumerable case, and explains why a unicity estimate neither certifies a reading nor guarantees an efficient solver.

Research Context

Attribution & Citation

CipherBank (Li et al., 2025) is a research benchmark studying LLM performance on cryptographic decryption tasks. Its reported results depend on model, prompt, algorithm and metric; they are not a measured accuracy result for this museum’s corpus or solvers.

Four claims that are not the same claim. An AI cipher result may be measuring metadata recognition (the filename gave it away), cipher-family classification (the ciphertext has a statistical signature, and the message stays unread), fixed-codebook recognition (a code pool the system was already trained on), or genuine plaintext and key recovery. Only the last is decipherment, and it is the one least often measured. The gap is quantifiable: the ACTS benchmark strips metadata in tiers and reports combined accuracy falling from 71.7% with metadata to 30.8% blind. Before benchmarking anything against this corpus, read the matched-input evaluation recipe and benchmark checklist — including the label-shuffled and length-only floors, and the rule that a deterministic stand-in for a model establishes nothing about a live one.
