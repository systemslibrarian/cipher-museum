Research and evidence

How a Decipherment Becomes Evidence

Compare Copiale, Mary Stuart and Z340, and test a Linear B structural hypothesis against a held-out record.

A decipherment is a chain of claims: source → transcription → structural hypothesis → key → reading → independent checks. These investigations distinguish historical evidence from museum exercises. Open the Evidence Workbench.

Copiale: the ordinary letters were the surprise

The Copiale manuscript combines familiar Roman letters with unfamiliar symbols. Knight, Megyesi and Schaefer first tested the familiar letters as the message-bearing alphabet. That hypothesis failed. Comparing neighboring contexts helped reveal groups of homophones among the unfamiliar signs; German language hypotheses and human interpretation guided the search.

  1. Source: preserve diacritics and distinguish symbol types before counting them. A familiar-looking letter need not play its familiar role.
  2. Competing hypothesis: treat ordinary Roman letters as ciphertext, then as separators. The successful reading uses unaccented Roman letters as word boundaries. Calling them meaningless nulls loses that function.
  3. Mixed key: the system includes multi-letter values such as CH, SCH and ST, logograms, and a colon that doubles the preceding consonant. A one-letter substitution cannot represent the whole manuscript.
  4. Evidence: test mappings across occurrences and read the resulting German in context. Some material remained unresolved in the 2011 paper; a successful passage does not certify every glyph.

Try it: in the workbench, enter an original exercise g1 g2 g3 g1 with g1→A, g2→CH and g3→space. Compare that typed key with forcing every sign to one letter. This exercise is invented; it is not a transcription from Copiale.

Source: Knight, Megyesi and Schaefer, “The Copiale Cipher” (2011), especially the transcription and decipherment discussion, PDF pp. 2–7. The paper and its manuscript figures supply the historical evidence.

Mary Stuart: preserve the dot, then test the story

The 57-letter collection published in 2023 contains more than 150,000 transcribed cipher symbols and about 50,000 plaintext words. Its 219 graphical symbol types include distinctions that cannot be compressed into a 26-letter alphabet.

  1. Source: record the archive, shelfmark and folio. The collection spans BnF Français 2988, Français 20506, Cinq Cent de Colbert 470 and Français 3158. Keep letter identity separate from a modern filename.
  2. Reading alternatives: diacritics distinguish symbols. If a mark might be a dot or damage, record both readings and its image region. Do not silently merge the two types to make a solver work.
  3. Language hypothesis: early Italian expectations gave way to French. A modern-English score is not evidence against a French reading.
  4. Search and interpretation: simulated annealing recovered letter homophones. Names, word codes, repeat and erase controls, and other nomenclature required further manual work. Preserve the literal reading before normalizing spelling or translating.
  5. Independent corroboration: surviving plaintext versions and historical correspondence help test the interpretation. They provide evidence beyond the language score used to select a candidate.

Try it: transcribe an invented pair as g17 g17-dot, save a span with both alternatives, and record why one is preferred. Revise the source and inspect the earlier annotation: its original location is retained. This tests provenance, not recovery of a historical letter.

Source: Lasry, Biermann and Tomokiyo, “Deciphering Mary Stuart’s lost letters from 1578–1584” (2023), §3.2, §4.2, §5.1 and Appendix A; PDF pp. 9–13, 16–26, 32 and 96–98. Figure 2 shows letter F38 and the importance of the source image.

Linear B: structure before sound

Linear B is a script, not a cipher. Nobody designed it to hide anything. Its signs record syllables and commodities in Mycenaean palace accounts, in an early form of Greek. When work began, both the sound values and the language were unknown, so there was no key to recover and no sender who wanted secrecy. Counting, grouping and testing predictions overlap with cryptanalysis; success meant a reading that independent evidence could check.

  1. Structure first. Alice Kober collected recurring words and showed that some appear in sets of three related forms, sharing a stem and changing their ending. She deliberately stopped before assigning sounds. From these relationships she drew up the beginning of a grid of signs that seemed to share a consonant or a vowel; Michael Ventris extended it.
  2. The structure outlived a wrong interpretation. Kober read her “triplets” as inflected forms of words. Ventris took a step she had not: he treated them as place names. Her first five proved to be the names of Cretan towns, with adjectives formed from them, and the relationships between signs that she had found still held.
  3. The expected language had to give way. Ventris’s working notes of 1951–52 tested Aegean and Etruscan parallels while Greek readings were still regarded as out of the question, and he favoured an Etruscan connection until mid-1952. He introduced his first full test of Greek, Work Note 20 of 1 June 1952, as a digression. Values from the grid then produced the names of Cretan towns and Greek words. The decipherment was announced on 1 July 1952.
  4. The test came from a tablet not used to build the values. Tablet Ta 641 was excavated at Pylos in 1952, and Carl Blegen studied it in spring 1953. Read with Ventris’s values, the words beside drawings of tripods and pots described those vessels: ti-ri-po-de beside tripods, qe-to-ro-we beside a four-handled pot, a-no-we beside one without handles. Blegen reported this in a letter of 16 May 1953. The tablet did not help form the values; it tested them.
  5. Some readings stay open. Linear B spelling leaves out l, r and s at the end of a syllable, and m and n before another consonant, so one written word can stand for different Greek words: pa-te can be patēr, “father”, or pantes, “all”. Context settles some cases: one tablet pairs pa-te with ma-te, “mother”, which points to patēr. Others remain open; even on Ta 641, Chadwick noted, the descriptions of the tripods allow several translations with no firm way to choose between them.
  6. No confidence score. Chadwick called the odds against Ta 641’s agreement arising by accident astronomical, and of a later check he wrote that such probabilities are “beyond mathematical analysis”. Ventris and Chadwick also pointed to the system’s success on texts found later. These are arguments, not measured probabilities, and this page does not turn them into a score.

    Maya glyphs: letters, syllables and dates

    Maya writing is a script for Maya languages, not a code. Mayan languages are living languages: the word read from the codex example below is still the word for turkey in modern Maya (Coe ch. 6). The glyphs stayed unread because scholars assumed the wrong kind of system, not because anyone hid their meaning.

    1. Source. Bishop Diego de Landa’s account of Yucatán, written in Spain around 1566, survives in a later copy that the abbé Brasseur found in the nineteenth century. It sets Maya signs beside Spanish letters (Coe ch. 4). The Maya codices add glyphs written beside pictures (Coe ch. 6).
    2. Transcription. Landa recorded what his Maya informants wrote when he said each Spanish letter aloud. A letter such as b was spoken as a syllable, “be”, and the sign written in answer recorded that sound (Coe ch. 6).
    3. Structural hypothesis. Taken at face value, the list is an alphabet: one sign for one letter. Readings built on that assumption failed, and Brasseur’s careless use of the list helped discredit phonetic approaches (Coe ch. 4). When Knorozov later proposed phonetic readings, Eric Thompson attacked them (Coe ch. 6).
    4. Key. Knorozov proposed in 1952 that most signs were syllables, a consonant plus a vowel, used alongside logograms that carry both meaning and sound. A word ending in a consonant is written with two syllables whose last vowel is silent, and that silent vowel usually echoes the first, a principle he called synharmony (Coe ch. 6).
    5. Reading. Over a picture of a turkey in a codex stand Landa’s sign for cu and a second sign. Read as syllables, k’u-tz(u) gives k’utz, the word for turkey in Colonial and modern Maya (Coe ch. 6). Later epigraphers refined the 1952 rule: an echoing silent vowel marks a short vowel, and a different one marks a long vowel, as in ba-k(i) for baak (Coe ch. 10).
    6. Independent checks. The picture beside the glyphs and the living language both check the reading from outside the script (Coe ch. 6). A separate check needed no phonetic reading at all. At Piedras Negras, Tatiana Proskouriakoff found that monuments fall into sets in which the earliest date, marked by a glyph she identified as birth, comes 12 to 31 years before a date marked by an accession glyph, and each set spans one ruler’s lifetime. The inscriptions record history, not only the calendar (Coe ch. 7).

    What stays open. Readings continue to be refined; the syllabic chart in Coe’s Appendix B records the values in use when the third edition appeared. The pronunciations used here are modern conventions.

      What the comparison teaches
      Copiale challenges the assumed role of a symbol. Mary Stuart challenges transcription, language and key structure. Z340 challenges reading order and makes exceptions visible. The Linear B analogy asks what recurring structure can establish before assigning sounds or meanings. Each case needs a different test. The workbench preserves those hypotheses and their evidence instead of treating readable output as a verdict.
