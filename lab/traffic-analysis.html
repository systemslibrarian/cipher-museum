A traffic-analysis exercise

Reading the Radio Log

Two days of invented wireless intercepts. You will not decrypt a single message. Who worked whom, which new callsign was which old station, and what can you honestly claim?

This log is fictional. Every callsign, frequency, time, bearing and fragment on this page is invented for teaching. It reconstructs a method, not any real network, army or day.

The method is traffic analysis: the study of the externals of communications—callsigns, frequencies, schedules, routing, volume and where a transmitter is—while message content is left to cryptanalysis. The National Security Agency’s history of the subject describes those elements and their wartime use; the page references below are to that history. Predict, then check: each step records what you chose, so that later evidence can show what your own choices imply. Everything runs in your browser.

1. Read the log

One invented net, two days. Our receiver listened from 06:00 to 18:00 each day, with the gaps shown. Each station transmits on its own frequency. At midnight every station took a new callsign. Serial numbers restart at 1 each day.

Groups is the length of the enciphered text. The text itself is not part of this exercise, so nothing can be decrypted. R followed by a number is a receipt for the other station’s message of that number.

    3. Day 2: the callsigns changed

    Changing callsigns was meant to break exactly the continuity you need. Analysts often bridged a change through whatever did not change at the same time: frequencies, contact schedules, an operator’s sending style, the pattern of who worked whom (NSA history, pp. 4–6). Match each Day 2 callsign to a Day 1 callsign, or leave it unresolved, then read what your matches imply.

    5. Second-stage evidence

    Two further sources arrive. Direction finding measures the bearing of a transmitter from a receiving post; bearings from two posts can cross to give an approximate fix (NSA history, p. 6). Operators’ chatter was generally sent unenciphered, and careless chatter could leak callsigns, schedules or even unit designators (p. 6). Record your Day 2 matches first, so the new evidence tests your own choices.

    On this invented 60 × 48 km grid, our two DF posts are on the west edge. The invented force faces west, so east is its rear. Each bearing is taken as accurate to ±2°, or ±4° for a very brief signal; the circle round each fix is a rough teaching estimate of that error, not a measured confidence region.

    After the exercise: the history behind it

    Callsigns are not units. The NSA history recounts how, on the day of Jutland in May 1916, an Admiralty operations director asked what a callsign located in harbour meant and was told it was the German fleet commander’s. The commander used a different callsign at sea; that one stayed at fleet headquarters. The British commander-in-chief was signalled that direction finding placed the German flagship in harbour (pp. 14–15).

    Quiet on the air is not quiet on the ground. In another First World War example, British signals suggested that Australian and Canadian units were still in Flanders while those units moved under signals silence to Amiens (p. 13). An absence of traffic says something about a radio, not necessarily about a unit.

    Volume is not intention. Volume was one of the externals analysts studied (p. 3), and in general more traffic allowed more to be inferred (p. 6). But a count of groups measures activity on the air. In this log the apparent Day 2 rise is mostly longer listening. A real rise could still be routine reports, repeats of garbled messages, an exercise, or dummy traffic sent to mislead (p. 8).

    Analysts were not working alone. Traffic analysis informed cryptanalysts about who was talking to whom, and its products were combined with decrypts and other sources (pp. 4, 8). James Thirsk’s chapter in The Bletchley Park Codebreakers is a participant’s account of this work as a wartime “log-reader”.

    How this differs from real traffic analysis

    • Scale. Real logs ran to thousands of entries across many nets. This one has 34 rows.
    • Callsign systems. Here callsigns change once. Real systems changed periodically to a prearranged plan, which analysts sometimes recovered and could then predict (p. 4).
    • Direction finding. The ±2° readings and fix circles are a rough model. Real bearings could be much worse, and a fix is an area, not a point.
    • Deception. Real targets changed procedures, reduced time on the air and sent dummy traffic (p. 8). This log contains no deliberate deception.
    • No answer key. Real analysts revised hypotheses as evidence accumulated and seldom learned for certain whether they were right.

    Where this leads

    The history route. Continue with Station HYPO, where partial JN-25 recovery worked alongside traffic analysis and operational context before Midway, or follow the Ciphers at War tour. The Hall of Codebreakers holds the people behind the methods.

    The modern version. Blind Relay, in Crypto Lab, runs the same move on modern logs. Its “Correlation without collusion” exhibit joins an Oblivious HTTP relay’s log to the gateway’s log without touching key material, on ciphertext size and on timing. Padding merges the size join into one anonymity set; when traffic is sparse, the timing join still links requests. Where sizes or arrivals coincide, it reports an anonymity set or an overlap instead of guessing, the same discipline as leaving a callsign unresolved here.
