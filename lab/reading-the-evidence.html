Four small investigations

Reading the Evidence

A promising key. An ordinary-looking word. A single misread letter. Test what each clue can tell you.

Predict what will happen, try it, then explain the result. The messages, keys and case notes in the four numbered activities are invented teaching examples. The linked papers supply the research ideas; these activities do not reproduce their datasets or trained models. A separate historical handbell case follows the exercises. Everything runs in your browser, with no upload or account.

How the experiment is measured

The generator encrypts with shift 7. The search receives only the ciphertext and selected vocabulary; it tries all shifts without being given the answer key. The original is used afterward to count edits. A correct key can coexist with an incorrect recovered message. Changing vocabulary can change a score without changing any letters in the source. No automatic spelling correction is applied.

Compare the older sentence under both vocabularies while leaving noise unchanged. Then change noise while keeping the sentence and vocabulary fixed. These comparisons separate model mismatch from a transcription error.

What does this reveal?

It records equal-symbol positions, not plaintext. Renaming is one-to-one: different symbols keep different names. In a homophonic cipher, different ciphertext symbols can still stand for the same plaintext letter, so this pattern is not necessarily the plaintext’s pattern. This does not apply as a decipherment method to modern encryption or a properly used one-time pad.

The single-occurrence action replaces the first repeated occurrence with a new symbol. If all symbols are distinct, it replaces the last with the first. With only one symbol, no equality relationship can change. Separating spaces here are token boundaries, not symbols being encoded.

Research credit: Nishant Kambhatla, Logan Born and Anoop Sarkar, Decipherment as Regression: Solving Historical Substitution Ciphers by Learning Symbol Recurrence Relations (2023), §3.1. This activity implements their first-occurrence numbering definition on an original example. It does not run their Transformer model or claim their decipherment performance.

The handbell: when a date rules out a cipher

Historical case — unresolved. Rudolf II’s alchemical handbell, made around 1600, carries a Greek-letter inscription. Bean, Gannon and Lang’s 2023 study corrects an earlier transcription and compares possible interpretations.

The same inscription, represented as digits or Latin letters, prompted different suggestions from Mason’s cipher classifier:

These tools used ACA-oriented training and English-language assumptions. Their rankings are clues, not calibrated probabilities of the bell’s cipher type.

Could Monome-Dinome explain an inscription from around 1600?

The paper dates Monome-Dinome in its present form to about 1936. That chronology rules out this particular suggestion for the bell. Eliminating a candidate does not identify the correct one.

Keep the question open: the paper does not settle whether the inscription encodes recoverable plaintext or represents another kind of meaningful symbolism. No decipherment is supplied here.

Source: Richard Bean, Corinna Gannon and Sarah Lang, The cipher of Emperor Rudolf II’s “Alchemical Hand Bell” (HistoCrypt 2023), pp. 13–17, §§2–3 and 5. The classifier reports and chronology come from that study; the museum has not inspected the artifact.

Sarah Lang’s Situating ciphers among alchemical techniques of secrecy (2023) adds a useful distinction: secrecy can involve cover names, symbolic conventions and social practices as well as reversible encryption. Transcription alone need not reveal meaning.
