What happens next?

Unidentified letters in a French archive restore a queen’s voice. A British admiral must reveal a German proposal without exposing his service’s access. A codebreaker reads a smuggler’s orders after the chance to intervene has passed.

Secrets of the Cipher Museum follows hidden messages into the decisions they make possible—and the questions they leave unresolved. The recovery of Copiale in 2011, the Zodiac 340 cipher in 2020 and Mary Stuart’s letters in 2023 brings the story into the work of modern researchers.

No mathematical background is needed. Eight original diagrams and six documentary images help where the method matters. Chapter links and QR codes lead to free exhibits where you can compare readings, test an assumption and inspect what a published recovery establishes. The historical narrative is complete without a browser.

Before you begin

A few questions about the book

How does it sit alongside The Code Book and The Codebreakers?

The question running through this book is what people could prove and do once they could read a secret. Mary Stuart’s correspondence frames the investigations, which reach the modern recoveries of Copiale, Z340 and her lost letters. Each chapter offers a related museum activity in reading, testing or judging evidence. Kahn and Singh are acknowledged predecessors in the notes.

Am I reading history or solving a mystery?

The prologue and twenty historical chapters are nonfiction, with sources and uncertainties identified. Four optional invented slips lead to The Curator’s Sealed Letter, a separate fictional case. You can enjoy the whole historical narrative without solving a puzzle.

What can I try before the book is published?

Trace the route of Mary’s intercepted correspondence, follow the two coded versions of the Zimmermann Telegram, or replay part of the published Z340 solution. The replay checks an existing solution; it does not identify the murderer. These exhibits are free, and teaching models are labelled.

From the prologue · Opening excerpt

The letters without a name

The letters were in the catalogue. Their author was missing.

Pages of cipher lay among manuscripts in the Bibliothèque nationale de France, near material concerning Italian affairs in the early sixteenth century. A catalogue could bring a researcher to those pages. It could not tell the researcher whose words were hidden there.

George Lasry, Norbert Biermann and Satoshi Tomokiyo were looking for historical ciphers they might decipher. The unfamiliar signs were enough to interest them. They began without a famous name to guide the search.

Italian seemed a reasonable language to try. It failed. French offered a better prospect, but the manuscript resisted the simplest approach. A mark might stand for a letter, a syllable or a whole word. Some marks contributed nothing to the reading. The team had to decide which differences in handwriting mattered before a computer could count the signs reliably.

Gradually, fragments of language emerged. They carried the grammar of a woman writing about confinement and negotiation. Names began to suggest a particular household, a particular ambassador, a particular prisoner. The collection had been placed in the wrong historical neighbourhood.

The researchers now had two problems. They needed to extend their key until the letters could be read. They also needed to establish that the person appearing through those readings was really the person who had sent them. A recognisable name could guide the work; it could also make a mistaken reading dangerously persuasive.

Somewhere else in the archives, earlier readers had left evidence that could test the answer. Those readers had entered the correspondence while its author was alive. They had been working for her enemies.

The modern recovery would become public in 2023. To understand what it returned to history, we must first visit a different interception, in 1586, when the delivery of a queen’s letter could help determine whether she lived.

For libraries, classrooms and curious friends

Read. Compare. Discuss.

A suggested 30-minute session, with an optional 15-minute puzzle warm-up. Use one shared screen or individual devices. No purchase, account or previous codebreaking experience is required.

  1. Read · 5 minutes. Read the historical opening excerpt. Which details are observations, and which questions still need an answer?
  2. Investigate · 15 minutes. Open the fictional Sealed Letter case and choose “Start the investigation.” Use its codebook to read the dispatch, then compare the four initial records. Keep the fictional case separate from the historical excerpt.
  3. Decide · 10 minutes. Choose one additional record before making a finding. Explain what it changes and what remains uncertain.

Optional warm-up · 15 minutes. Try the four slips before entering the case. Use hints or answer reveals whenever helpful; solving them is optional.

Questions for discussion

  1. What can a catalogue description tell you—and how could a reading overturn it?
  2. What changes when the same message survives in two versions?
  3. Which statement in the fictional case is an observation, and which is an inference?
  4. What one additional source would most improve your confidence?

Work individually or in pairs. Finish by naming one claim the documents support and one they leave unresolved.
